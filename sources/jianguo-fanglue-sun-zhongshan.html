Plans for National Reconstruction (Jianguo fanglüe 建国方略) — Sun Zhongshan 孙中山

What this is

Sun Zhongshan (孙中山; also Sun Wen 孙文 / Sun Yat-sen) gathered under the umbrella title 建国方略 (Jianguo fanglüe, Plans for National Reconstruction) three major constructive programs written across the late 1910s and early 1920s — psychological reconstruction, material (industrial) reconstruction, and social (civic / parliamentary) reconstruction — together with a short 附编 of party-state foundational texts. Where the companion source 三民主义 (1924 Guangzhou lectures) states the Three Principles as public doctrine, 建国方略 is the how-to corpus: how to break the old “knowing is easy, doing is hard” mental block; how to build ports, railways, industry, and mines on an international-development scale; and how ordinary citizens are to run meetings so that “a sheet of loose sand” becomes organized public power.

This file is a 2012 simplified-Chinese ebook (AZW3 / Kindle MOBI lineage, chenjin5.com free-book imprint via Anna’s Archive). Body text is clean digital Chinese (not OCR of a scan). Commercial interstitial pages appear at the head of the package and do not cut the three main books.

Chapter breakdown

Style: English title + (pinyin + 中文) + multi-line content gloss grounded in this edition’s openings. Glosses track what the text argues in its own time, not later party doctrine.

Book I — Psychological reconstruction (Xinli jianshe 心理建设 / Sun Wen xueshuo 孙文学说)

Preface (Zixu 自序). Sun recounts thirty-odd years of running the national cause: the overthrow of Qing autocracy and founding of the republic should have opened onto the revolutionary party’s 三民主义, 五权宪法, and the construction templates of the older Geming fanglüe 革命方略. Instead, after the first success, party men called his claims “too idealistic” for China; as provisional president his influence was weaker than as revolutionary leader. Removing one Manchu autocracy produced “countless bandit autocracies,” worse for the people’s livelihood. He locates the intellectual disease in the proverb 知之非艰，行之惟艰 (“to know is not hard; to act is hard”) and announces the counter-thesis 行之非艰，知之惟艰 — doing is not hard; knowing is hard — as the key that unlocks construction.

Ch. 1 — Proof from eating and drinking (Yi yinshi wei zheng 以饮食为证). At the hinge from destruction to construction, critics invoke “knowing easy, doing hard.” Sun turns from Wang Yangming’s “unity of knowledge and action” to a long demonstration that everyone does diet every day while almost no one knows the chemistry, physiology, and hygiene of food — proof that action can be universal while knowledge remains rare and late.

Ch. 2 — Proof from using money (Yi yongqian wei zheng 以用钱为证). Money is not an innate faculty but a civilizational habit. All civilized people use it constantly, yet few can define what money is. Sun reviews Chinese and Western accounts of currency as medium and standard (“中准”), and the long history from cowries and metals to modern money — again, universal practice with scarce true knowledge.

Ch. 3 — Proof from composition (Yi zuowen wei zheng 以作文为证). Chinese civilization has exalted letters for millennia; ability in prose became a false universal competence while other arts decayed. Yet the script tradition is rich, durable, and assimilative (Japan, Korea, Vietnam as “同文”). New-learning proposals to abolish Chinese writing are rejected. Composition is something many do; grammar, rhetoric, and the science of language are things few know.

Ch. 4 — Proof from seven further matters (Yi qishi wei zheng 以七事为证). Extends the case beyond diet, money, and writing to building, shipbuilding, city walls, canals, electricity, chemistry, and evolution: crafts and sciences long practiced before theorized; foreign engineers design while Chinese laborers execute in the treaty-port landscape — knowledge and action divided among persons.

Ch. 5 — General theory of knowledge and action (Zhixing zonglun 知行总论). With ten proofs, the ancient “know easy, act hard” and Yangming’s “unity” are overturned as guides for national work. Mencius is cited for people who act and practice without understanding the Way. Yangming’s motive (urge people to do good) is granted; his formula still teaches fear of the hard and dampens action.

Ch. 6 — If one can know, one can act (Neng zhi bi neng xing 能知必能行). In a scientific age, large works (wireless, aircraft, continental railways, Suez and Panama) succeed when image, plan, and execution follow knowledge. Sun maps his own 革命方略 periods — 军政, 训政, 宪政 — as planned stages of destruction and construction once the doctrine is known.

Ch. 7 — One can act even without knowing (Bu zhi yi neng xing 不知亦能行). Objection: if science must come first, enrichment waits a century of schooling. Answer: most human advance begins in 不知而行 — drill, experiment, exploration, adventure. Scientific knowing is powerful but historically late; construction cannot wait for universal prior knowledge.

Ch. 8 — Where there is will, there is achievement (You zhi jing cheng 有志竟成). Affairs that match principle, popular feeling, world tide, and group need, once willed by the first to know, do not fail. Sun corrects earlier caution in Kidnapped in London and narrates the revolutionary origin from personal resolve through 兴中会 to 同盟会, overseas Chinese, and inland martyrs — memory as encouragement that construction too can be finished.

Book II — Material reconstruction (Wuzhi jianshe 物质建设 / Shiye jihua 实业计划)

Preface (Zixu 自序). Written as the European war ended: use wartime-scale machines and organized labor for China’s industrial leap and to absorb demobilized foreign labor. Powers instead demobilized industry; China lost a chance, while the world remained in economic distress. Development of China’s resources is a world problem; 发展之权，操之在我则存，操之在人则亡 — the right to develop, if held by us, means survival; if held by others, ruin.

Opening essay (Pianshou 篇首). Daily war expenditure and the “second industrial revolution” of state-scaled production leave surplus plant and labor when fighting stops. International joint development of China is framed as reconstruction for the powers and a path out of overproduction — the six plans follow.

Plan I (Di yi jihua 第一计划). Dual track: personal enterprise (encourage, legal protect, scrap suicidal taxes, fix currency, clear official barriers, ease transport) vs state enterprise for monopolistic or non-delegable works, using foreign capital and skilled organizers under training-for-succession terms. Four principles: most profitable routes for capital; what the people need most; least resistance; suitable location. Centers on a 北方大港 (Northern great port) and supporting systems (rail into the northwest, colonization, canals, iron/coal).

Plan II (Di er jihua 第二计划). 东方大港 (Eastern great port) as counterpart center — five parts: the eastern port (argument that a planned port on Hangzhou Bay near Zhapu outranks merely enlarging Shanghai), regulating the Yangzi waterway and banks, inland river ports, improving existing Yangzi routes and canals, and large cement works.

Plan III (Di san jihua 第三计划). 南方大港 at 广州 to complete the three first-class seaports: improve Guangzhou as world port; improve the Guangzhou water system; 西南铁路系统; coastal commercial and fishery ports; shipyards. Hong Kong’s rise after the Opium War is treated as having taken Guangzhou’s harbor role; republican “awakening” is to reverse that through Chinese public works.

Plan IV (Di si jihua 第四计划). Detail of the 100,000-mile railway vision: central, southeast, northeast, expanded northwest, and plateau systems, plus locomotive and rolling-stock works — knitting ports to interior and to Mongolian / Xinjiang / highland frontiers.

Plan V (Di wu jihua 第五计划). After key and fundamental industries, develop 本部工业 (consumer and comfort goods industries) so higher wages meet cheaper necessities and comforts; critiques the idea that China is a “cheap living” country when measured in labor hours rather than money.

Plan VI (Di liu jihua 第六计划). Mining as root of machine civilization: iron, coal, oil, copper, special minerals, mining machinery, smelting plants — government coordination of a still-young Chinese mining sector.

Conclusion (Jielun 结论). Three world problems — international war, commercial war, class war — are answered in this international development scheme through 互助 (mutual aid) rather than Darwinian struggle. Warns that China, as the most populous country, would again be the stake of great-power conflict if development is left to partition logic.

Appendices 1–6 (Fulu 附录). Supporting letters, documents, and technical notes attached to the industrial plan in this edition (international and engineering correspondence of the Shiye jihua dossier).

Book III — Social reconstruction (Shehui jianshe 社会建设 / Minquan chubu 民权初步)

Preface (Xu 序). China has the greatest people and richest land yet lagged Japan’s transformation because 人心涣散 — hearts scattered, strength uncondensed. Manchu bans on assembly, literary inquisition, and punishment for private talk stripped meeting, press, and thought freedoms for two and a half centuries. After foreign winds, Meiji example, and revolutionary shock produced the Republic, ambition still tried to restore empire (洪宪). The Republic’s safety now depends on developing 民权. Citing Lincoln’s government of/by/for the people, Sun presents this book as a primer of meeting procedure (after Western parliamentary practice) so four hundred million cease to be loose sand.

Juan 1 — Forming assemblies (Juan yi jiehui 卷一结会). Ch. 1–4: definition of a meeting (three or more under rules); need for officers and order versus the Chinese habit of unstructured talk; types (temporary meetings, committees, permanent societies); how to organize, elect, and keep records.

Juan 2 — Motions (Juan er dongyi 卷二动议). Ch. 5–10: the three-step spine of business — motion, debate, vote; six formal steps from rising and addressing the chair through announcement of the result; wording, seconding, withdrawal, division of questions, and related practice.

Juan 3 — Amendments (Juan san xiuzhengan 卷三修正案). Ch. 11–13: nature and effect of amendments; must be germane; primary/secondary amendment practice; ways to amend (insert, strike, substitute) with worked examples (e.g. a local self-government society hunting a hall).

Juan 4 — Order of motions (Juan si dongyi zhi shunxu 卷四动议之顺序). Ch. 14–18: independent vs subsidiary motions; seven common subsidiary motions and their precedence (including lay on the table, previous question / stop debate, commit, amend, postpone, etc.); privileged motions (adjourn, recess, questions of privilege).

Juan 5 — Questions of privilege and order (Juan wu quanyi ji zhixu wenti 卷五权宜及秩序问题). Ch. 19–20: points that interrupt ordinary motion order — privilege and order — when they may be raised, how the chair handles them, and limits (including relative to adjournment).

Conclusion and appendix. Closes the primer and adds model 章程, narrative illustrations of full meetings, and 表决 tables — teachable templates for societies and public bodies.

Supplement (Fubian 附编)

Manifesto of the First National Congress of the Chinese Kuomintang (Zhongguo Guomindang di-yi ci quanguo daibiao dahui xuanyan 中国国民党第一次全国代表大会宣言). Opens on China’s present: revolution from Jiawu through Gengzi to Xinhai overthrew monarchy but did not complete the passage from one-nation domination to multi-national equality, from autocracy to popular rights, from handicraft to capitalist production, or from semi-colony to independence. Diagnoses warlord and imperialist structure and states the reorganized party’s political program in the mid-1920s united-front setting.

National Government Outline of National Reconstruction (Guomin zhengfu jianguo dagang 国民政府建国大纲). Twenty-five articles: government builds the Republic on 三民主义 and 五权宪法; 民生 first (food, clothing, shelter, movement); then 民权 (election, recall, initiative, referendum under tutelage); then 民族 (domestic weak peoples to self-determination; resist foreign aggression and revise unequal treaties). Construction in three periods — 军政, 训政, 宪政 — with county autonomy as the unit, a constitution drafted after enough counties/provinces are ready, and final power in the people under five-yuan government.

Premier’s Testament (Zongli yizhu 总理遗嘱). Dated Minguo 14.2.24 (24 February 1925). Forty years for China’s freedom and equality; awaken the masses and unite with nations that treat China as equal; comrades must follow 建国方略, 建国大纲, 三民主义, and the First Congress manifesto; hasten a national assembly and abolition of unequal treaties. Signed 孙文.
