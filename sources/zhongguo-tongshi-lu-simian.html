A General History of China (Zhongguo tongshi 中国通史) — Lü Simian (吕思勉)

Lü Simian (吕思勉, 1884–1957) wrote this two-book general history for university teaching while Shanghai was the wartime “isolated island” (gudao 孤岛). Kaiming shudian (开明书店) issued the upper volume (上册) in 1940 and the lower volume (下册) about four years later; the file on the shelf is the 2009 Shanghai guji (上海古籍出版社) printing in the Lü Simian wenji (吕思勉文集) series (ISBN 9787532552795). The book’s own design is double: the upper book is a cultural / institutional history in the spirit of older 典制 (dianzhi, treatises on institutions), eighteen topical chapters from marriage and clan to religion; the lower book is a political narrative from the origins of the Chinese people through successive courts to China on the road of revolution. The author states in the prefatory matter that the two faces must be kept together — institutions without the political sequence, or politics without the social frame, would each be one-sided.

This source page narrates what the book contains. OCR from the scanned PDF is uneven (many pages show spaced or misread characters); structure and titles below follow the edition’s table of contents as recovered from the extract and the standard chapter order of this work, with English glosses and romanization throughout.

How the book is built

Author’s preface (zixu 自序) and introduction (xulun 绪论) set the problem. Drawing the old distinction associated with Ma Duanlin’s (马端临) Wenxian tongkao preface — historical matter as two great classes — Lü wants both the change of political order through time and the standing arrangements of society, family, office, tax, arms, letters, and belief. The introduction opens onto definitions of history, the place of Chinese history, methods of study, and how this book is staged. Body prose in readable stretches discusses, for example, marriage moving from capture and exchange toward settled household forms, and xuanju (选举, selection/recommendation, later overlapping the examination path) as against pure hereditary office — with an explicit comparison that Han-era selection was a Chinese institutional resource Western antiquity lacked in the same shape.

Upper volume (上册) — Chinese cultural / institutional history

Eighteen topical chapters. Each runs a theme across periods rather than staying inside one dynasty. English titles below are descriptive glosses of the Chinese heads. Expanded notes follow the edition’s institutional agenda (standing arrangements of family, office, tax, arms, letters, and belief).

1. Marriage (hunyin 婚姻)

Household formation from capture, exchange, and bride-service toward settled, ritualized, often patrilocal marriage. Tracks women’s early economic standing in agricultural communities and how the marriage bond becomes the book’s first social institution. The chapter moves from plunder and exchange of women through service marriage into full family-centered li, with the classical six rites (from nacai to qinying) as the mature form. Wife vs concubine status, three-month completion of the bond, and divorce rules show how economic motives survive under Confucian ritual gloss.

2. Clan and lineage system (zuzhi 族制)

Surname and lineage, mourning grades, succession and adoption. Kin order is treated as the underwriting of local authority and a permanent frame for later political life, not a side topic of anthropology. Joint family ideals, ancestral ritual, and lineage corporations bind living members and property across generations. Lineage power can stabilize villages yet also shelter private force and oligarchy against the written law of the court.

3. Form of government (zhengti 政体)

From early collective and aristocratic arrangements toward imperial autocracy. Recurring structural choice between feudal enfeoffment (fengjian 封建) and commandery-county (junxian 郡县) rule — the same polarity the lower book will stage in political narrative. Distinguishes wang and jun, chen and min, and how “public realm” language shrinks while loyalty-to-one-house hardens after Han. Late critical recovery of older democratic residues (especially Ming–Qing thought) sits against the long practice of monarchical concentration.

4. Social ranks and classes (jieji 阶级)

Free and unfree, great lineages and commoners, conquest strata, and later status groups. Feeds directly into nine-rank and great-family problems of the early medieval courts when the lower book reaches Wei–Jin. Statutory rank, mean-status barriers, and military or ethnic privilege cut across simple rich/poor maps. Mobility through office, money, and examination never fully erases the inherited ladders the chapter catalogs.

5. Property (caichan 财产)

Land, movable wealth, and the legal-social defense of private holding as the root of settled order. Property here is institutional history, not a modern economic treatise. Partition, sale, mortgage, and concentration of land dominate the agrarian story; slaves, cattle, and commercial capital appear where they matter. Tax capacity, rebellion, and reform programs all hinge on who holds title and how the state can reach it.

6. Official system (guanzhi 官制)

Central and local offices, growth of bureaucratic specialization, and how posts are stacked from court to county. The standing machinery that any dynasty must staff and reform. Maps the long shift from feudal officers to commandery–county administrators and a six-ministry-style center with censors and provincial layers. Nominal rank vs real commission (chaiqian), sinecure, and duplication are standing late-imperial problems.

7. Selection and recommendation (xuanju 选举)

How men are chosen for office: ancient recommendation below hereditary great posts; Han pathways; the long road toward examination (keju 科举). The book insists selection institutions matter as much as dynastic luck — and compares Han-era selection as a Chinese resource Western antiquity lacked in the same shape. Qualification grids, patronage, and avoidance rules try to balance “promote talent” against faction and local capture. Hereditary limit at the top and elective or exam methods below remain the deep tension across ages.

8. Taxes and levies (fushui 赋税)

Grain, labor, cloth, and money dues; how fiscal extract matches land and household registers. Cross-dynastic spine for later crises when registers break or rates harden. From early tribute and ideal well-field talk through poll, land, and twice-tax regimes toward commutation in silver (Single-Whip-type logic). Irregular surcharges and local squeeze often outweigh statute rates in the lived peasant burden.

9. Military system (bingzhi 兵制)

Who fights, how troops are raised and fed, and the link between arms and social stratum — from early levies toward professional and household-soldier forms treated again in the political half. Rotational service, hereditary military households, farmer-soldier systems, and hired or dependent corps each solve manpower differently. When finance and command split from the center, eunuchs, governors, and frontier abutments rewrite who really holds force.

10. Criminal law (xingfa 刑法)

Codes, punishments, and the moral-political theory of law in successive regimes. Law as state craft and ethical order, not a bare list of penalties. Five punishments, redemption, collective liability, and graded Ming–Qing codes show continuity under changing labels. Adjudication layers, evidence norms, and amnesty are political tools as much as pure justice.

11. Industry and livelihood (shiye 实业)

Agriculture, crafts, and occupations as the material base beneath food, housing, and money chapters. Livelihood ties the institutional survey to everyday production. Mining, salt, and specialized crafts sit beside the farm; state monopolies compete with private shafts and workshops. Kaogong-style hereditary skill memory and later market crafts both count as “industry” in Lü’s broad productive sense.

12. Money and currency (huobi 货币)

Coin, bullion, and credit practices across the imperial long run. Currency history as a thread of state capacity and market life. Debasement, multiple standards, cloth units, paper notes, and silver’s late dominance recur as management failures. Currency chaos hits prices, military pay, and—by the Qing–modern hinge—foreign silver trade flows.

13. Clothing and food (yishi 衣食)

Material culture of dress and diet as historical evidence — what people wore and ate as a record of rank, region, and change, not antiquarian aside. Staples (millet, wheat, rice), salt and tea, and later New World or commercial crops thicken the table over time. Sumptuary dress marks rank; luxury bans repeatedly lose to commercial cloth and fashion.

14. Dwelling and travel (zhuxing 住行)

Houses, roads, water transport, and the geography of everyday movement. Settlement and mobility as the spatial face of the institutional order. Urban wards, courtyard houses, and capital plans sit beside cart, boat, and courier networks that integrate the realm. Passes, hostels, and shipping risk limit both commerce and the real reach of officials.

15. Education (jiaoyu 教育)

Schools, teachers, and the training that feeds selection and scholarship. Bridges xuanju above and xueshu below. State schools and private academies alternately serve and criticize the court; exam culture warps curricula toward classics and set forms. Thin technical schooling and uneven literacy remain standing limits on “educating the realm.”

16. Language and writing (yuwen 语文)

Speech and script as tools of administration and culture — the medium through which law, learning, and empire are written. Multi-dialect speech under a classical written standard; philology and rhyme books try to discipline the written word. Late pressure toward clearer vernacular writing sits beside the unbroken prestige of the literary language.

17. Learning and scholarship (xueshu 学术)

Classics, histories, masters, and technical learning in the broad “great culture” sense. Scholarship as a standing institution parallel to office and cult. Han learning, Song lixue, evidential research, and late synthesis mark major climate shifts; print multiplies texts after Tang–Song. Substance vs empty talk, and Buddhist–Daoist challenge, repeatedly force redefinition of what counts as true learning.

18. Religion (zongjiao 宗教)

Cults, imported faiths, and the place of belief beside the state and the lineage. Closes the upper book by seating religion inside the same institutional long view as marriage and tax. Popular worship, state sacrifice, and Daoist/Buddhist institutions rarely remade material life the way modern Western civilization would. The upper book thus ends on civilizational stability about to meet global contact in the lower narrative’s final arc.

Together these eighteen chapters are the book’s cultural history half — wide enough that later readers call the scope a “great culture” (da wenhua 大文化) survey, while the text itself stays in the institutional and social vocabulary of its own day.

Lower volume (下册) — Chinese political history

A continuous political narrative from ethnic origins to the early revolutionary decades. Chapter titles follow the standard order of this tongshi; a few middle heads are lightly normalized where scan OCR breaks characters. Each chapter below is given a multi-line gloss (title + several lines of content) in the same style as the upper book.

Origins and antiquity (chs. 19–25)

19. Origins of the Chinese people (Zhongguo minzu de youlai 中国民族的由来).
How the book frames the formation of the people later called Han and their neighbors — ethnic starting point for the political half, not a separate ethnology. Multiple ancient stocks and long mixing replace any single pure-origin myth; border groups enter and exit the named core over time. Sets the recurring theme that “China” absorbs and renames peoples across the narrative that follows.

20. Chronology of Chinese history (Zhongguo shi de niandai 中国史的年代).
Dating systems and the skeleton of eras so the narrative sequence that follows can be placed on a usable timeline. Reign titles, cyclical signs, and the gap between legendary king lists and datable history are handled as method, not antiquarian sport. Gives the lower book its working clock from deep antiquity to the revolutionary decades of Lü’s own horizon.

21. Archaic opening (gudai de kaihua 古代的开化).
Early cultural opening and sage-king narrative space — the “opening of antiquity” before dense king-list politics. Tools, settlement, and moral-technical advances are condensed in culture-hero memory (fire, farming, writing, order). Bridges pre-dynastic haze to the first named houses without pretending full archival clarity.

22. Affairs of Xia, Yin (Shang), and Western Zhou (Xia Yin Xizhou de shiji 夏殷西周的事迹).
Political deeds of the Three Dynasties arc as the lower book tells them: rise, succession, and the Zhou feudal settlement that later ages remember as classical order. Shang cult and bronze power, then Zhou kinship kingship and enfeoffment hierarchy, become the model later courts invoke. Decline of royal grip and the slide toward Spring and Autumn multipolarity close the arc.

23. Spring and Autumn–Warring States rivalry and Qin unification (Chunqiu Zhanguo de jingzheng he Qin guo de tongyi 春秋战国的竞争和秦国的统一).
Hegemons, multi-state struggle, and the Qin drive that ends the classical multi-polity world — hinge into imperial time. Iron, mass infantry, bureaucratic extraction, and the hundred schools run beside diplomacy and total war. Ends in Qin unification (221 BCE) as the structural break into empire.

24. Ancient assimilation of alien peoples (gudai duiyu yizu de tonghua 古代对于异族的同化).
How early regimes absorb or transform neighboring groups — a standing problem that will return with the Hu, Khitan, Mongols, and Manchus. Conquest, marriage, office, and cultural prestige work unevenly; assimilation at the edges is often two-way. Prefigures later dual patterns of Sinicization under Chinese courts and under conquest dynasties.

25. Survey of ancient society (gudai shehui de zongshu 古代社会的综述).
Pulls the archaic political story back toward the upper book’s social themes before the narrative enters Qin–Han institutions of empire. Class, land, family, and state from the cultural half are restated as one ancient-period snapshot. Serves as a deliberate pause before Qin’s sharp institutional break.

Qin–Han imperial foundation (chs. 26–32)

26. Qin policies for ruling All under Heaven (Qin chao zhi tianxia de zhengce 秦朝治天下的政策).
Commandery-county rule, standardization, and the hard package of measures by which Qin tries to govern a newly unified realm — and why that package cannot stabilize long. Unified graphs and measures, law-first governance, large works, and suppression of classicist opposition define the template. Short dynasty, long shadow: Han keeps the shell of unity while rewriting the spirit of rule.

27. Feudal-polity reaction between Qin and Han (Qin Han jian fengjian zhengti de fandong 秦汉间封建政体的反动).
After Qin’s fall, partial revival of enfeoffment and the early Han settlement that mixes princely kingdoms with commanderies — a structural “reaction” before full recentralization. Cadet kingdoms threaten the throne; from Gaozu through Jing to Wu the court recentralizes step by step. “Reaction” names the swing back to fiefs—and the counter-swing that restores unitary commandery rule.

28. Emperor Wu of Han — internal and external affairs (Han Wudi de neizheng waijiao 汉武帝的内政外交).
Fiscal and institutional tightening at home; war and diplomacy abroad (notably the Xiongnu problem). Marks the high imperial style of Western Han state power. Salt-iron and related monopolies, elevated Confucian curriculum, and Western Regions policy redefine ambition. Costs of glory—strain on people and later retrenchment—open the dynasty’s long back half.

29. Decline and fall of Former Han (Qian Han de shuaiwang 前汉的衰亡).
From peak Western Han into succession crises, consort-kin politics, and the hollowing that opens the door to Wang Mang. Local great families and weakened center prepare usurpation even while the imperial name still stands. Ends in the crisis that lets the Xin house claim the Mandate.

30. Rise and fall of the Xin house (Xin shi de xingwang 新室的兴亡).
Wang Mang (王莽): usurpation, reform program, and collapse — treated as a full political episode, not a footnote between two Hans. Classical revival reforms, currency and land experiments, and popular revolt (including Red Eyebrows) destroy legitimacy. Collapse returns the field to Liu restorers and the wars that birth Later Han.

31. Rise and decline of Later Han (Hou Han de xingshuai 后汉的兴亡).
Eastern Han restoration under Guangwu, then long slide through eunuch and consort-kin conflict toward irreparable centre weakness. Early recovery and reopening sit beside later factional paralysis at Luoyang. Yellow Turbans and regional armies end effective second-Han unity.

32. Later Han partition and the Three Kingdoms (Hou Han de fenlie he Sanguo 后汉的分裂和三国).
Warlord partition and the Wei–Shu–Wu settlement that ends the first long imperial unity and opens the age of division. Military-aristocratic politics replace a single court; famous campaigns cannot overcome structural fragmentation. Jin’s costly short unification is already foreshadowed as fragile.

Division, North China under the Hu, Sui–Tang (chs. 33–40)

33. Jin circumstances (Jin chao de xingshi 晋朝的形势).
Western Jin’s brief unity and structural fragility — the court situation on the eve of the disorders that wreck the north. Household registers, great-lineage dominance, and hollow military arrangements make unity brittle. Sets why a restored name of empire cannot hold the north once crisis breaks.

34. Opening of the Five Hu disorders (Wuhu zhi luan de kaiduan 五胡之乱的开端).
Inland settlement of non-Han groups, breakdown of Jin control, and the first phase of the Five Hu upheaval that empties the old heartland order. Migration, garrison politics, and elite miscalculation turn settled “insiders” into conquerors. Mass displacement toward the Huai and Yangzi begins the dual north–south story.

35. The Five Hu disorders (continued / settlement) (Wuhu zhi luan 五胡之乱).
Northern regimes in succession; wreck and partial restabilization of North China under Hu and hybrid courts. “Disorder” is also competitive state-building amid extreme violence and ethnic hierarchy experiments. Locks in a long structure: conquest North versus émigré South.

36. Beginning and end of the Northern and Southern dynasties (Nanbeichao de shimo 南北朝的始末).
Full north–south parallel dynasties until Sui closes the split — political map of the long dual age. Parallel institutions, intermittent war, and deep cultural exchange persist despite political division. Prepares the ground for Sui reunification after centuries of bipolar China.

37. Outer-frontier situation from the Northern–Southern age through Sui–Tang (Nanbeichao Sui Tang jian saiwai de xingshi 南北朝隋唐间塞外的形势).
Steppe and frontier peoples as the external frame for reunification and High Tang power — saiwai (塞外) pressure and opportunity together. Rouran, Turks, and northeastern configurations force diplomacy, marriage alliance, and frontier systems. Sui–Tang grandeur is inseparable from managing this outer map.

38. High age of Sui and Tang (Sui chao he Tang chao de shengshi 隋朝和唐朝的盛世).
Sui bridge and Tang florescence: restored unitary empire at its institutional and military high point. Canals, codes, exams, fubing-type forces, and cosmopolitan capitals mark the plateau. The brilliance is real—and time-limited before mid-dynasty shock.

39. Mid-Tang decline (Tang chao de zhongshuai 唐朝的中衰).
An–Shi and after: the turn from florescence into structural mid-dynasty decline without yet total collapse. Provincial military governors, hollowed finances, eunuchs, and court factions erode central grip. Culture may still flower while the political skeleton weakens.

40. Tang collapse and Shatuo intrusion (Tang chao de shuaiwang he Shatuo de qinru 唐朝的衰亡和沙陀的侵入).
Final Tang breakdown and Shatuo (沙陀) entry into the succession struggles that feed the Five Dynasties. Huang Chao and warlord courts open the field; non-Han cavalry power becomes kingmaker inside China. Opens the tenth-century world of short dynasties and lasting northern pressure.

Five Dynasties to Ming–Qing (chs. 41–49)

41. Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms; Khitan intrusion (Wudai Shiguo de xingwang he Qidan de qinru 五代十国的兴亡和契丹的侵入).
Rapid northern dynasties, southern kingdoms, and Khitan (Qidan 契丹) pressure — the trough before Song recentralization. Sixteen Prefectures and peer diplomacy with Liao rewrite older tributary habits. Song founding inherits a map already shaped by Khitan strength.

42. Cultural transformation of China in Tang–Song times (Tang Song shidai Zhongguo wenhua de zhuanbian 唐宋时代中国文化的转变).
Hinge chapter where political narrative and the upper book’s cultural themes meet again — Tang-to-Song as a civilizational turn, not only a dynastic handoff. Print, examinations, Neo-Confucian ethics, and a more civilian upper class change what the society rewards. Framed against long cycles of northern pressure and selective assimilation.

43. Structural weakness of Northern Song (Bei Song de jiruo 北宋的积弱).
Song’s rebuilt centre under the permanent marks of accumulated weakness (jiruo 积弱) against Liao/Jin and in fiscal-military structure. Wealth and letters without matching cavalry dominance; reform drives expose elite splits more than they fix the frontier. “Jiruo” is institutional, not a lack of commerce or culture.

44. Southern Song restoration blocked by circumstance (Nan Song huifu de xingge shijin 南宋恢复的形格势禁).
Why recovery of the north stays blocked: geography, balance of forces, and court choices after the Jingkang disaster. Peace and war factions contest a rich but truncated empire; Jiangnan prosperity funds defense more than reconquest. Ends under Mongol pressure the South cannot ultimately absorb.

45. Founding of the Mongol empire (Menggu diguo de jianli 蒙古帝国的建立).
Rise of the Mongol imperial structure that will overrun Xia, Jin, and Song and open Yuan rule over China. Steppe unification, organization, and use of sedentary specialists explain unprecedented Eurasian reach. China becomes one theater inside a larger imperial system.

46. Han restoration enterprise (Hanzu de guangfu shiye 汉族的光复事业).
The drive that ends Yuan and opens Ming — framed in the book’s vocabulary of recovery (guangfu 光复) of Han rule. Resistance, collaboration, and demographic shock accompany the fall of Mongol dominion in China proper. Zhu Yuanzhang’s success is cast as restoration of a Han political center, not only a new dynasty name.

47. Ming rise and decline (Ming chao de shengshuai 明朝的盛衰).
From Ming founding strength through the long internal arc of prosperity and decay before the final Ming–Qing crisis. Autocratic hardening, eunuchs, fiscal strain, and frontier military households mark the curve. Commercial growth and high culture coexist with political brittleness.

48. Ming–Qing rise and fall (Ming Qing de xingwang 明清的兴亡).
Conquest transition: late Ming collapse and Qing seizure of the Mandate as one linked political movement. Rebel paths, Manchu banners, and northern elite collaboration shape the new multiethnic empire. Southern resistance wars prolong the transition without reversing the outcome.

49. Qing rise and decline (Qing dai de shengshuai 清代的盛衰).
High Qing expansion and order, then the turn into nineteenth-century structural decline that the final chapters will detail. Kang–Yong–Qian inner-Asian empire and population boom precede rebellion and external shock. “Shengshuai” compresses glory and the long slide into one dynastic curve.

Early modern contact and the revolutionary road (chs. 50–54)

50. Early Sino–Western dealings (Zhong Xi chuqi de jiaoshe 中西初期的交涉).
Sea and land routes; Portuguese Macao; Dutch and others; the paired themes trade and mission (tongshang chuanjiao 通商传教) as the book’s own summary of first contact; military and technical asymmetry that reframes later Qing crises. Canton-system habits and tributary psychology meet expanding European company and state power. Sets the board for unequal conflict once industrial-military gaps become undeniable.

51. Han restoration movements (Hanzu de guangfu yundong 汉族的光复运动).
Late-imperial risings framed as recovery movements in the book’s political vocabulary — popular and elite challenges inside the Qing order. Secret-society memory, Ming-loyalist residue, and modern racial-national rhetoric against Manchu rule feed the same word guangfu. Becomes major emotional fuel for late Qing activism (with all its simplifications).

52. Qing disorder and crisis (Qing chao de luanwang 清朝的乱亡).
Nineteenth-century breakup: internal rebellion and external shock as the dynasty enters terminal disorder (title normalized where OCR splits the pair 52–53). Taiping, Nian, and related wars force regional armies that save the court while hollowing the center. Disorder is both social explosion and mutation toward warlord patterns.

53. Fall of the Qing (Qing chao de fuwang 清朝的覆亡).
End of the dynasty proper — the political closure of imperial time before the revolutionary road chapter. Reform and constitutional last tries fail to hold the provinces; 1911 exits and abdication follow. The imperial narrative ends faster than a new political culture can be built.

54. China on the road of revolution (Geming tuzhong de Zhongguo 革命途中的中国).
Early Republic and the unresolved revolutionary path. Closes the lower book where the upper book’s institutional long view meets twentieth-century rupture — wartime composition horizon, not a full PRC narrative. Opens with the proverb that great vessels finish late: decades after 1911 still count as mid-revolution. Yuan Shikai, party struggles, and unfinished transformation leave the story on the road, not at a completed new order.

What this source is for in the wiki

Use Zhongguo tongshi when the question is how a mid-Republican professional historian laid out the whole of Chinese history for students: institutions first as standing problems (marriage to religion), then political sequence from ethnic origin to revolution. It pairs with Qian Mu’s Guoshi dagang (国史大纲) — another wartime university outline, more essayistic and periodized by “shifts” — and with the Qingshi gao (清史稿) draft, which is a dynastic gao for the Qing alone rather than a tongshi. Prefer Lü for cross-dynastic institutional themes (tax, arms, selection, clan) and for a compact political spine; prefer the Qingshi gao for Qing reign-by-reign and biographical bulk; prefer Qian for the moral-periodization voice of the dagang.

Limits of this extract

  • Scan OCR is lossy: many pages show inter-character spaces, substituted glyphs, and broken heads in the middle of the lower volume. Do not quote fine wording from the raw file without checking a cleaner edition.

  • The 2009 wenji typesetting may include modern editorial punctuation; the wartime argument and chapter plan remain those of Lü’s general history.
