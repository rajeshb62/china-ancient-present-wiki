The Great Revolutionary Sun Yat-sen (Dagemingjia Sun Yixian 大革命家孙逸仙) — Miyazaki Tōten 宮崎滔天, trans. Huang Zhonghuang 黄中黄

What this is

大革命家孙逸仙 (Dagemingjia Sun Yixian, “The Great Revolutionary Sun Yat-sen”) is a Chinese edition of the celebrated Japanese revolutionary sympathizer Miyazaki Tōten’s (宮崎滔天, also 白浪庵滔天) account of Sun Yat-sen, translated by 黄中黄 (Huang Zhonghuang). It belongs to the body of early twentieth-century literature in which Japanese and Chinese activists exchanged accounts of the Chinese revolutionary movement. The work derives from Miyazaki’s memoir 《三十三年之夢》 (“Thirty-three Years’ Dream”) and presents Sun as the founder-leader of the revolutionary party, set against the broader theme of whether China could be reborn through revolution.

The present file is a republication of the translation — a “第A05版 (新闻·真相)” / 都市女报 newspaper-style scan of a DuXiu-sourced document (77 image pages). It opens with a series of prefaces (黄中黄自序, a Chinese sympathizer’s 序, 滔天原序, and 滔天原自序) followed by a chaptered narrative.

Prefaces

黄中黄自序 (translator’s preface). Written in the voice of the translator, dated 共和 (Minguo-equivalent dating in the OCR reads “共和…千六百十四年…月二十日” — the standard 1903 date of the translation). It argues that Sun Yat-sen is not merely a private person but a newly discovered name of new China — that “without Sun Yat-sen China cannot be reborn,” and that what is named 孫逸仙 is the substance through which China’s renewal is to be achieved. It frames the work as a correction to those who would dismiss Sun and as a statement of the connection between Sun and China’s future.

序 (by a Chinese sympathizer). Recalls that four years earlier Sun seemed to Cantonese merely a “sea pirate of the Guangzhou bay,” then recounts how Miyazaki Tōten, the Japanese “eastern-sea knight,” sought out revolutionary talent in China, encountered Sun, and came to revere him — positioning Sun as one who, before the Qing’s corruption was even exposed (before 甲午/乙未, 1894–95), staked his life on restoring the fatherland, bearing the competition of the race, and proclaiming the rights of man.

滔天原序 (Miyazaki Tōten’s preface, signed 孫文逸仙 拜序). A preface that in Miyazaki’s framing casts him as a latter-day eastern knight (東海俠客) who, like a patron of a new 李世民, travels China to find heroes — here Sun — to build a great undertaking for Asia and for the survival of the Yellow race. It speaks of Miyazaki returning from his journeys to record what he saw, for those concerned with the fate of Asia.

滔天原自序 (Miyazaki Tōten’s own preface). Miyazaki’s personal statement of purpose: he embraces the “world as one family” ideal, holds that true ideals are realizable (not dreams), and declares himself a world revolutionary and a social revolutionary. He argues that land is not man-made but heaven’s gift to all, and therefore should not be monopolized by a few — the theoretical ground he says compelled him to act. He records his resolve to use China as the base of “strength” for realizing his ideals, and the price he paid: the Philippine affair, imprisonment in Singapore, expulsion from the Straits Settlements and from Hong Kong, the abortive revolutionary armies, the 孫逸仙 (Sun Yat-sen) affair, and the internal dissension among comrades — all of which he says he set down in 《三十三年之夢》, for “life is a dream.”

Chapter breakdown

Style: English title + (pinyin + 中文) + multi-line gloss of the chapter’s content as recovered from the OCR body.

第一章 — Sun Yat-sen’s career and his revolution (孫逸仙之略歴及其革命)

Opening on Sun as 興中會首領 (leader of the Xingzhonghui / Revive China Society), the chapter narrates Sun’s early revolutionary career — his organizing, the pre-甲午 context, and the 1896 London legation imprisonment (使館之囚), a set-piece of Sun’s biography. It pairs 陳白 (Chen Shaobai, 陳少白) with 白浪滔天 (Miyazaki) as the associates who aided Sun, then moves to reflective essays: 支那國民與共和政治 argues about the Chinese people and republicanism; 共和政治須統一英雄之方法 reflects on how republican rule must unify “heroes”; 支那之興亡與人道之消長 ties China’s fortunes to the fortunes of humanity; and 品會楊之比較 compares figures (likely Sun / Kang circles or revolutionary organizations) as a set of contrasts.

第二章 — Sun’s party and Kang’s party (孫黨與康黨)

The chapter sets Sun Yat-sen’s revolutionary party (孫黨) against Kang Youwei’s (康有為) reformist/monarchist party (康黨). It opens on 康有為, then 革命之急先鋒 (the vanguard of revolution) and 戊戌事變與康黨 (the 1898 Reform Movement and its coup, and Kang’s party’s fate). 康有為之來 recounts Kang’s arrival abroad; 滔天之革命論 gives Miyazaki’s own theory of revolution; 革命與改革之比較 contrasts revolution and reform; 保皇亦須流血 argues that even monarchism would require bloodshed. 康孫聯合之夢想 treats the once-hoped union of Kang and Sun; 康有為與西后 and 萬木草堂 touch Kang’s relation to the Empress Dowager and his famous academy; 衣帶詔與…電 alludes to the purported secret edict and telegrams; 小國會 a “small parliament.” The chapter closes on the split: 康絕孫 (Kang breaks with Sun), 日本人之康 (the Japanese and Kang), 康拒孫 (Kang rejects Sun), and 康有為與李鴻章 (Kang and Li Hongzhang).

第三章 and onward — The storm in the South Seas (南洋之風雲)

The narrative moves to the overseas Chinese communities of Southeast Asia and the revolutionary fundraising and organizing among them. The OCR body shows episodes of Miyazaki’s and Sun’s activity in Hong Kong, the Straits Settlements, and the South Seas — including confrontations with colonial authorities (the Singapore imprisonment and expulsion, the Hong Kong expulsion mentioned in the prefaces), and the condition of the revolutionary forces. Later pages record the abortive uprising attempts and the difficulties of coordinating secret societies (三合會, 哥老會) with the revolutionary program.

Relation to the other sources

This is the second foreign-authored account of Sun Yat-sen on the shelf, alongside 孫逸仙傳記:

SourceAuthor / angle
三民主義, 建国方略Sun’s own program (1924)
孫逸仙傳記 (trans. 徐植仁, 1946)Western biography translated into Chinese
大革命家孙逸仙 (this file)Japanese revolutionary sympathizer’s (宮崎滔天) contemporary account, translated 1903

Where 孫逸仙傳記 is a full life told by an outside Western observer, 大革命家孙逸仙 is a contemporary Japanese voice writing in the 1890s–1900s, close to the events and to Sun himself — valuable for the early revolutionary network, Sun–Kang rivalry, and the pan-Asian dimension. Together the two biographies and the two doctrinal texts give the wiki both the doctrine and multiple contemporary framings of Sun.
