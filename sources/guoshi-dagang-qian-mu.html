Outline of National History (Guoshi dagang 国史大纲) — Qian Mu (钱穆)

What this book is

Guoshi dagang (国史大纲, Outline of National History) is Qian Mu’s (钱穆, courtesy name Binsi 宾四) single-volume university-level outline of Chinese history written as a gangmu (纲目) textbook: bold gang (纲, main heads) carry the narrative skeleton; indented notes and inline parentheses supply evidence, dates, and asides. The draft digital object here is the two-volume complete set (quan 2 ce 全2册) from the Qian Mu xiansheng quanji (钱穆先生全集 / 钱宾四先生全集) new collated edition (xin jiaoben 新校本) published by Jiuzhou chubanshe (九州出版社, Beijing), ISBN 9787510809941 — a mainland reset of the Taiwan Lianjing (联经) collected-works text.

The book’s origin, as the edition’s chuban shuoming (出版说明, publisher’s note) recounts, is Qian’s Beijing University (Beijing daxue 北京大学) Chinese general history course (Zhongguo tongshi 中国通史) from autumn Republic year 22 (民国二十二年, 1933). After years of rotating specialists, the university asked one lecturer to hold the full survey. Qian taught four years at Beida, then two more at Southwest Associated University (Xinan lianda 西南联大) after the Lugouqiao (芦沟桥, Marco Polo Bridge) crisis of 1937 drove the northern schools south. Colleague Chen Mengjia (陈梦家) urged a textbook for the wartime generation. From May 1938 to June 1939 Qian wrote in seclusion at a villa by Yanquan xiasi (岩泉下寺) outside Yiliang (宜良), Yunnan, using thick notebooks of primary excerpts he had accumulated since Beida. The manuscript — eight bian (编, parts), forty-six zhang (章, chapters), roughly half a million characters — went to Shangwu yinshuguan (商务印书馆, Commercial Press). Lü Simian (吕思勉), Qian’s former middle-school teacher, did a final Shanghai proof; he praised especially the northern–southern economic transfer chapters and the long arc from Wei–Jin tuntian (魏晋屯田, military-agricultural colonies) through Tang zuyongdiao (租庸调). Censorship delayed first issue until June 1940 (Republic 29): reviewers wanted “Hong-Yang zhi luan” (洪杨之乱) rewritten as “Taiping tianguo” (太平天国); Qian refused wholesale renaming and kept the draft wording. Early copies reached Free China via Haiphong; later a Chongqing “national-crisis” (guonan 国难) edition circulated.

This is not a multi-juan dynastic zhengshi (正史). It is a synthetic national outline from deep antiquity through the Anti-Japanese War and the opening of the Republic, written so a student can see one continuous body (yi da ti 一大体) — institutions, economy, thought, and “international” pressure — without drowning in person-by-person annals.

How the book asks to be read

Before the long yinlun (引论, introduction) stand four “beliefs” the reader is asked to hold: a citizen should know the country’s past; knowledge should carry wenqing yu jingyi (温情与敬意, warmth and respect); one should reject blank nihilism toward that past and the pose of standing at history’s absolute summit; national “improvement” without those habits is likened to the reform of a conquered or semi-colonial people. The yinlun itself (~20,000 characters) states method: hunt the locus of change in each age (Warring States → thought; Qin–Han → political institutions; Wei–Jin → social economy), because “where change is, there is historical spirit.” Form is deliberately spare textbook gangmu, not exhaustive chronicle.

Front matter in this EPUB also includes the Jiuzhou xin jiaoben note, the long chuban shuoming on composition and print history, and editorial matter on maps by Wang Hui (王恢) and the advice to pair the outline with Qian’s Zhongguo wenhua shi daolun (中国文化史导论, Introduction to the cultural history of China). Beida’s old Zhongguo tongshi cankao cailiao (中国通史参考材料) of primary extracts is not reprinted in the collected works by Qian’s wish.

Part I — Archaic culture; the Three Dynasties

Di yi bian 第一编　上古文化三代之部

Chapter 1 — Cradle of Zhongyuan Huaxia culture; beginnings of Chinese history; Yu–Xia
Zhongyuan Huaxia wenhua zhi faxiang; Zhongguo shi zhi kaishi; Yu Xia shidai（中原華夏文化之發祥；中國史之開始；虞夏時代）

Opens the national story in the Central Plains (Zhongyuan 中原) under the label Huaxia (華夏). Sections move from modern exploration of high antiquity (Paleolithic / Neolithic / Yinxu 殷墟 finds as the book frames them) to the geographic “birth” of that culture, then Xia (夏) king-lists and calendrical estimates, then major Xia events. The tone is outline-critical: what can be said from tradition and from early twentieth-century archaeology, not a romance of sage-kings alone.

Chapter 2 — A new dynasty on the lower Yellow River: Shang–Yin
Huanghe xiayou zhi xin wangchao; Yin Shang shidai（黃河下游之新王朝；殷商時代）

Shang / Yin (商 / 殷) royal genealogy and chronology; conjectures on settlement geography; cultural inferences (bronze-age elite world as the outline needs it); Yin–Zhou (殷周) relations as the hinge to the next order.

Chapter 3 — Rise of the feudal empire: Western Zhou
Fengjian diguo zhi chuangxing; Xizhou xingwang（封建帝國之創興；西周興亡）

Western Zhou (周) king-list; early fengjian (封建, enfeoffment) — including classic eastern placements such as Lu (鲁) and Qi (齐) that push Zhou power toward the sea, and Jin (晋) on the old Xia ruins as a north–south corridor; continued eastward expansion; killing of Youwang (幽王) and Pingwang’s (平王) move east (dongqian 东迁) that opens the Spring and Autumn world.

Part II — Spring and Autumn and Warring States

Di er bian 第二编　春秋战国之部

Chapter 4 — The age of ba politics: Spring and Autumn
Bazheng shiqi; Chunqiu shimo（霸政時期；春秋始末）

Periodization of Chunqiu (春秋): pre-hegemon decades; the long ba (霸, hegemon) run from Qi Huan (齐桓) and Jin Wen (晋文); later decline into dafu zhizheng (大夫执政, ministerial rule); a closing sketch of general cultural conditions. Classic themes such as rangyi (攘夷, resisting “barbarian” pressure) appear as structural headings inside the outline.

Chapter 5 — A new landscape of military-state struggle: Warring States
Junguo douzheng zhi xin jumian; Zhanguo shimo（軍國鬥爭之新局面；戰國始末）

Zhanguo (战国) chronology and stages; the shift from zongfa fengjian (宗法封建, kin-based enfeoffment) to the xin junguo (新军国, new military state) — manpower, law, territory, and diplomacy as one package of change.

Chapter 6 — Rise of free learning among the people: pre-Qin masters
Minjian ziyou xueshu zhi xingqi; Xian Qin zhuzi（民間自由學術之興起；先秦諸子）

For Qian, the deepest Zhanguo change is private, non-temple scholarship. Spring and Autumn learning is still guizu xue (贵族学, aristocratic learning) rooted in zongfa (宗法), ancestral temples, and the shi (史) / temple invocator as the old “only scholar.” Then Ru (儒, classicists) and Mo (墨) rise; academic orientations turn; “shi spirit” runs high; nobles keep guests (yangxian 养贤); among commoner thinkers the outline sorts types (ascetic Mo-line, non-office “slick” scholars, legal-technocratic office-seekers / zongheng 纵横, righteous Ru office, etc.). This chapter is the book’s charter for treating thought as historical structure, not ornament.

Part III — Qin and Han

Di san bian 第三编　秦汉之部

Chapter 7 — Creating the unified government: Qin rise and fall; Han beginnings
Dayitong zhengfu zhi chuangjian; Qin dai xingwang yu Hanshi chuqi（大一統政府之創建；秦代興亡與漢室初起）

Qin–Han king-lists; “coagulation” of state and people; first appearance of a unified government and its collapse; emergence of a pingmin zhengfu (平民政府, commoner-staffed regime) under early Han. Institutional creation is stressed: dismantling fengjian, establishing junxian (郡县, commandery–county), melting aristocratic vs commoner estates as pure legal casts.

Chapter 8 — Civil evolution of the unified government: from Han Wudi to Wang Mang
Tongyi zhengfu wenzhi zhi yanjin; you Han Wudi zhi Wang Mang（統一政府文治之演進；由漢武帝至王莽）

Social baseline of early Western Han (farmers and nubi 奴婢 still near older burdens: shui 税 / rent, yi 役 / labour, fu 赋 / military levies). Early government and scholars; beginnings of a civil ideal at the centre; Han Wu (汉武) “fugu genghua” (复古更化, restore antiquity / renew transformation); a shiren zhengfu (士人政府, literati government) from Zhao–Xuan–Yuan–Cheng; Han Ru political thought; Wang Mang (王莽) receiving Zen abdication and reforming — including the outline’s critical notes on speed, personnel, and missing “party” support for ideals.

Chapter 9 — Decay of unified government: Eastern Han
Tongyi zhengfu zhi duoluo; Donghan xingwang（統一政府之墮落；東漢興亡）

Eastern Han emperors and calendar; the imperial house; waizi (外戚, consort kin) and huanguan (宦官, eunuchs); how each group entered the throne’s body politic.

Chapter 10 — New place of the shi clans: rise of Eastern Han great houses
Shizu zhi xin diwei; Donghan mendi zhi xingqi（士族之新地位；東漢門第之興起）

Expanding political weight of shizu (士族); chaju (察举) and zhengbi (征辟) recruitment; Taixue (太学) and qingyi (清议, pure criticism); the danggu (党锢) proscriptions; how mendi (门第, great-family rank) hardens; manners of Eastern Han shi houses.

Chapter 11 — The unified government facing outward: Qin–Han power and foreign situations
Tongyi zhengfu zhi duiwai; Qin Han guoli yu duiwai xingshi（統一政府之對外；秦漢國力與對外形勢）

Compares Western and Eastern Han strength; Western Han and the Xiongnu (匈奴); Eastern Han and the Western Qiang (西羌).

Part IV — Wei, Jin, Northern and Southern Dynasties

Di si bian 第四编　魏晋南北朝之部

Chapter 12 — Long division begins: the Three Kingdoms
Changqi fenlie zhi kaishi; Sanguo shidai（長期分裂之開始；三國時代）

After Eastern Han breaks, the outline opens the long Wei–Jin–Nanbeichao decline: old court power fails, centrifugal warlords rise, new regimes stay dark, and thought finds no exit. Sanguo (三国) king-lists close the chapter as the start of multi-century split rather than a short heroic digression.

Chapter 13 — Afterglow of unified government: Western Jin
Tongyi zhengfu zhi huiguang fanzhao; Xijin xingwang（統一政府之迴光返照；西晉興亡）

Western Jin’s brief reunification is treated as afterglow, not recovery: weak throne, inland settlement of non-Han groups, capture of Huai and Min emperors, wreck of the old cultural centre, and entry of new religions as the unitary order finally dies.

Chapter 14 — New ground on the Yangtze: Eastern Jin southward
Changjiang liuyu zhi xin yuandi; Dongjin nandu（長江流域之新園地；東晉南渡）

With the north lost, the Yangtze basin becomes the main stage of “traditional China.” Eastern Jin king-list; repeated northern expeditions that never restore lasting unity; civil strife that eats the same energy that might have retaken the Central Plains.

Chapter 15 — Long northern chaos: Five Hu and Sixteen Kingdoms
Beifang zhi changqi fenluan; Wuhu shiliuguo（北方之長期紛亂；五胡十六國）

Tables and sketches of the Five Hu (Wuhu 五胡) and Sixteen Kingdoms: who settled where, how regimes succeeded one another, and the twin themes of sinicization and Hu–Han cooperation that the outline will carry into the Northern dynasties.

Chapter 16 — Southern dynasties sink: Song, Qi, Liang, Chen
Nanfang wangchao zhi xiaocheng; Nanchao Song Qi Liang Chen（南方王朝之消沉；南朝宋齊梁陳）

Southern Song–Qi–Liang–Chen king-lists; rotten courts and kin-killing; decline of southern mendi prestige. Against the north’s later recovery, the south is judged weaker in political vitality despite holding the cultural capital of the old elite.

Chapter 17 — New life for northern regimes: the Northern dynasties
Beifang zhengquan zhi xin shengming; Beichao（北方政權之新生命；北朝）

From Tuoba Wei through Eastern/Western Wei to Northern Qi and Northern Zhou: northern Han-ization and the Confucian scholarly line; Xiaowen’s (孝文) move of the capital; civil gains under Qi and Zhou that prepare Sui reunification.

Chapter 18 — Transformed feudal force: Wei–Jin–Nanbeichao mendi
Bianxiang de fengjian shili; Wei Jin Nanbeichao zhi mendi（變相的封建勢力；魏晉南北朝之門第）

Jiupin zhongzheng (九品中正) and great-family rank; decay of schools and open exams; southern immigrant qiaoxing (侨姓) versus native Wuxing (吴姓); marriage and status rules; northern junxing / guoxing (郡姓 / 国姓). Mendi (门第) is framed as a new quasi-feudal order after classical enfeoffment had already died.

Chapter 19 — Social form under that force (I): Western Jin and the south
Bianxiang de fengjian shili xia zhi shehui xingtai (shang); zai Xijin ji Nanchao（變相的封建勢力下之社會形態（上）；在西晉及南朝）

Late-Han desolation; changing farmer status; Western Jin household levies and official-rank land limits (guanpin zhantian 官品占田); southern immigrant great houses; soldiers’ legal status and burdens.

Chapter 20 — Social form under that force (II): Five Hu and the north
Bianxiang de fengjian shili xia zhi shehui xingtai (xia); zai Wuhu ji Beichao（變相的封建勢力下之社會形態（下）；在五胡及北朝）

Northern agrarian shares under the Five Hu; Northern Wei juntian (均田); Western Wei fubing (府兵) as institutional answers that later feed Sui–Tang machinery.

Chapter 21 — Spread of religious thought: antiquity through Northern–Southern Dynasties
Zongjiao sixiang zhi miman; shanggu zhi Nanbeichao zhi zongjiao sixiang（宗教思想之瀰漫；上古至南北朝之宗教思想）

Ancient cult change; Eastern Han Daojiao (道教) and recipe arts; Wei–Jin–Nanbeichao Buddhism; northern Dao–Buddhist clashes; a forward glance to Sui–Tang sinicization of Buddhist learning — religion as a structural mark of the long decline age.

Part V — Sui, Tang, Five Dynasties

Di wu bian 第五编　隋唐五代部

Chapter 22 — Unified florescence returns: Sui rise and fall; early Tang
Tongyi shengyun zhi zailin; Suishi xingwang ji Tang chu（統一盛運之再臨；隋室興亡及唐初）

Sui wealth, population, and granaries; Yangdi’s overreach; Tang founding and the Zhenguan (贞观) settlement as the second great unitary florescence after Qin–Han.

Chapter 23 — Political machinery under the new florescence: High Tang government
Xin de tongyi shengyun zhi xia zhi zhengzhi jigou; sheng Tang zhi zhengfu zuzhi（新的統一盛運之下之政治機構；盛唐之政府組織）

Rebuilding zaixiang (宰相) power after centuries of drift; refurbishing local government so the centre can again rule a whole empire without Wei–Jin style decay at the counties.

Chapter 24 — Social conditions under florescence: jinshi, fubing, and farmers
Xin de tongyi shengyun xia zhi shehui qingtai; sheng Tang zhi jinshi fubing yu nongmin（新的統一盛運下之社會情態；盛唐之進士府兵與農民）

Gongju (贡举) paths — pupils, prefectural tribute, decree exams — with jinshi (进士) supreme; zuyongdiao (租庸调) fiscal package; fubing (府兵) military households. Institutions read as the social face of High Tang strength.

Chapter 25 — Decay inside florescence (I): tax and military systems loosen
Shengyun zhong zhi shuai xiang (shang); Tangdai zushui zhidu yu bingyi zhidu zhi feichi（盛運中之衰象（上）；唐代租稅制度與兵役制度之廢弛）

When the spirit behind the rules fades, zuyongdiao slides toward liangshuifa (两税法); fubing yields to fangzhen (方镇) and palace armies. Institutional death is treated as loss of creating consciousness, not mere bad luck.

Chapter 26 — Decay inside florescence (II): officials and shi rot
Shengyun zhong zhi shuai xiang (xia); Tangdai zhengfu guanyuan yu shiren zhi fuhua（盛運中之衰象（下）；唐代政府官吏與士人之腐化）

Unlimited opening of office through exams and other doors; swelling of government posts; the same civil machinery that made florescence becomes overcrowded and soft.

Chapter 27 — Outward posture under florescence: early Tang arms and later threats
Xin de tongyi shengyun xia zhi duiwai zitai; Tang chu wugong ji zhongye yihou zhi waihuan（新的統一盛運下之對外姿態；唐初武功及中葉以後之外患）

Early success against Türks and other frontiers; the turn after An–Shi (安史) when foreign pressure and internal militarization reverse the High Tang external advantage.

Chapter 28 — A great age sinks: mid–late Tang politics and society
Da shidai zhi moluo; Tang zhongye yihou zhengzhi shehui zhi ge fangmian（大時代之沒落；唐中葉以後政治社會之各方面）

Fanzhen (藩镇) governors — often of non-Han origin — hold the provinces after An–Shi; the capital no longer commands the map. First half of the late-Tang diagnosis.

Chapter 29 — A great age sinks (continued)
Da shidai zhi moluo (xu)（大時代之沒落（續））

Palace eunuchs, court factions, and scholar-official party strife complete the internal picture: military provinces outside, closed and divided centre inside.

Chapter 30 — Dark shock: Huang Chao and the Five Dynasties / Ten Kingdoms
Hei’an shidai zhi da dongyao; Huang Chao zhi luan yiji Wudai shiguo（黑暗時代之大動搖；黃巢之亂以及五代十國）

Huang Chao (黄巢) and the fall of Tang; Wudai shiguo (五代十国); rise of the Khitan (Qidan 契丹); misery of the Central Plains; north–south split as structure again — the trough before Song recentralization.

Part VI — The two Song

Di liu bian 第六编　两宋之部

Chapter 31 — A poor-and-weak new centre: early Northern Song
Pinruo de xin zhongyang; Bei Song chuqi（貧弱的新中央；北宋初期）

Song king-list; rebuilding central power after the Five Dynasties; external weakness and internal poverty as paired structural traits; further institutional weak points that no early reform fully cures.

Chapter 32 — Literati self-awareness and reform: Qingli and Xining
Shidafu de zijue yu zhengzhi gexin yundong; Qingli Xining zhi bianfa（士大夫的自覺與政治革新運動；慶曆熙寧之變法）

Intellectual dawn among Song shi; Qingli (庆历) reforms; Xining (熙宁) New Policies under Wang Anshi (王安石) — reform as the self-conscious politics of a literati age.

Chapter 33 — Old and new party strife; northern and southern talents after Yuanyou
Xinjiu dangzheng yu nanbei rencai; Yuanyou yixia（新舊黨爭與南北人才；元祐以下）

New-party southern tilt; Luo–Shu–Shuo (洛蜀朔) opinion blocs; moral language of “upright vs wicked” that turns policy dispute into permanent faction war.

Chapter 34 — North–south split again: Song, Liao, and Jin war and peace
Nanbei zai fenlie; Song Liao Jin zhi hezhan（南北再分裂；宋遼金之和戰）

Jin destroys Liao; Jin destroys Northern Song; Southern Song–Jin war and peace; Southern Song finance; Liao / Jin / Song king-lists as scaffolding for the second long division of the millennium.

Part VII — Yuan and Ming

Di qi bian 第七编　元明之部

Chapter 35 — The storm arrives: Mongol conquest
Baofengyu zhi lailin; Menggu ruzhu（暴風雨之來臨；蒙古入主）

For Qian, the first time the whole country falls under a non-traditional alien regime. Steps against Xia, Jin, and Song; Yuan political climate; taxes and economic policy; armies and bans; clergy; scholars and the examination system under Mongol rule.

Chapter 36 — Traditional politics revived as monarchical dictatorship (I): Ming rise and fall
Chuantong zhengzhi fuxing xia zhi junzhu ducai (shang); Mingdai xingwang（傳統政治復興下之君主獨裁（上）；明代興亡）

Ming king-list; “worsening” of traditional politics into personal monarchy; abolition of the chancellorship; grand secretaries and eunuchs as the substitute centre.

Chapter 37 — Traditional politics revived as monarchical dictatorship (II)
Chuantong zhengzhi fuxing xia zhi junzhu ducai (xia)（傳統政治復興下之君主獨裁（下））

Early Ming “good institutions” and their later rot — the second half of the dictatorship diagnosis after the throne has already absorbed the old ministerial apex.

Chapter 38 — North–south transfer of economy and culture (I): Tang to Ming society
Nanbei jingji wenhua zhi zhuanyi (shang); zi Tang zhi Ming zhi shehui（南北經濟文化之轉移（上）；自唐至明之社會）

Economic and cultural maps; north–south political geography and population rise and fall — the opening block of the long transfer Lü Simian later praised.

Chapter 39 — North–south transfer (II)
Nanbei jingji wenhua zhi zhuanyi (zhong)（南北經濟文化之轉移（中））

Yellow River disasters in the north; damage from outside peoples and bad government that hollows the old heartland.

Chapter 40 — North–south transfer (III)
Nanbei jingji wenhua zhi zhuanyi (xia)（南北經濟文化之轉移（下））

Water control and development in Jiangsu–Zhejiang south — the constructive counterpart to northern decline, completing the Tang-to-Ming economic pivot.

Chapter 41 — Free private teaching rises again: Song–Yuan–Ming learning
Shehui ziyou jiangxue zhi zai xingqi; Song Yuan Ming sandai zhi xueshu（社會自由講學之再興起；宋元明三代之學術）

After aristocratic mendi fade, Song–Ming intellectual spirit, academies and lectures, and scholar-led social projects reopen a space of private teaching comparable, in Qian’s arc, to the pre-Qin masters.

Part VIII — Qing

Di ba bian 第八编　清代之部

Chapter 42 — “Narrow tribal regime” rebuilt (I): Qing entry
Xiayi de buzu zhengquan zhi zaijian (shang); Qingdai ruzhu（狹義的部族政權之再建（上）；清代入主）

Manchu rise to the pass (Jianzhou Jurchens, Nurhaci’s inherited armours, Seven Great Grievances, Ming military disaster); late Ming rebels; Southern Ming resistance — conquest as the return of a “narrow tribal” form of rule.

Chapter 43 — “Narrow tribal regime” rebuilt (II)
Xiayi de buzu zhengquan zhi zaijian (xia)（狹義的部族政權之再建（下））

Qing king-list; policy toward Han; institutions of the conquest regime; military expansion that secures the High Qing map.

Chapter 44 — Literati morale under that regime: scholarship before mid-Qing peak
Xiayi de buzu zhengquan xia zhi shiqi; Qingdai Qian–Jia yiqian zhi xueshu（狹義的部族政權下之士氣；清代乾嘉以前之學術）

Ming remnant integrity; Qian–Jia (乾嘉) philological climate; later divorce of politics from learning as morale and public purpose thin.

Chapter 45 — Popular upheaval under that politics: mid–late Qing disorders
Xiayi de buzu zhengzhi xia zhi minbian; Qing zhongye yixia zhi bianluan（狹義的部族政治下之民變；清中葉以下之變亂）

Peak-to-decline after Qianlong–Jiaqing; Hong–Yang / Taiping narrative under Qian’s own labels; Xiang and Huai armies and Tongzhi “restoration” as military answers that cannot fully reset the regime type.

Chapter 46 — Discard the old, open the new: Qing fall and Republican founding
Chu jiu yu kai xin; Qingdai fuwang yu Minguo chuangjian（除舊與開新；清代覆亡與民國創建）

Late Qing political scene; self-strengthening; abolition of keju (科举) and new schools; Wuxu (戊戌) and Xinhai (辛亥); post-1911 politics; cultural and social revolution; Sanmin zhuyi (三民主义) and war of resistance / national construction; closing vision of Chinese culture’s “new world mission” after victory — the outline’s wartime horizon, not a post-1949 continuation.

Edition and limits of this file

  • Author: Qian Mu (钱穆), 1895–1990.
  • Composition: 1938–39 (Yiliang); first Shanghai Commercial Press printing 1940; many later Taiwan / Hong Kong / mainland resets.
  • This package: Jiuzhou “new collated” complete 2 volumes inside the collected-works series (EPUB via Anna’s Archive).
  • Coverage end: Late Qing → early Republic and Anti-Japanese War horizon (ch. 46), not PRC institutional history.
  • Extraction caveat: EPUB vendor watermarks (“ePUBw.COM”) remain in places; body prose is continuous traditional-character scholarly Chinese with gangmu typography.
