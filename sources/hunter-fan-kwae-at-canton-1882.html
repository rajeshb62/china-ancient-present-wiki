The ‘Fan Kwae’ at Canton before Treaty Days, 1825–1844 — William C. Hunter

What this is

William C. Hunter (1812–1891), writing as “an old resident,” reconstructs the closed Canton of the Thirteen Factories as he knew it from the mid-1820s to his departure in 1844. The title’s fan kwae (番鬼, “foreign devil / foreign barbarian”) is the local name for the small community of Western merchants allowed to live on the Pearl River (珠江 / “Choo”) bank outside the city walls. The preface (Paris, 31 March 1882) states the frame: since 1745 no port but Canton had been open; no foreigner might enter the country or even the city; treaties have so changed business and daily life that the old conditions should now be “placed on record.”

This is a continuous memoir, not numbered chapters. Running heads read OLD CANTON. Printed body runs pp. 1–157, then a short Epilogue naming Robert B. Forbes of Boston as the encourager of the book. The Kegan Paul publisher’s catalogue after the colophon is not part of the memoir.

Companion volume in this wiki: Bits of Old China (1885), the same author’s later sketch-book of the same world.

Walk through the book

Preface

The Middle Kingdom “deigned to suffer” a few foreign barbarians in factories originally meant one per nationality, divided by three narrow streets from the suburbs to the river. No other port since 1745. Treaties have ended that mode of business; the old life is here set down.

Voyage of the Citizen (1824)

Departure from New York is still a spectacle. The ship Citizen (498 tons, Capt. E. L. Keen) is one of seven owned by Thomas H. Smith, China merchant. Cargo: 350,000 Spanish dollars in kegs (no London letters of credit yet), furs, lead, bar and scrap iron, quicksilver. Crew of thirty-two plus two officers; several have served in the last war with Britain or on the U.S. 74 Franklin. Passenger Hunter is bound for Smith’s Canton factory; a second passenger, surgeon Fullerton of the English East India Company (known on board as “Doctor Smyth”), later takes two “golden lilies” from Macao toward England as a speculation and is last seen as surgeon of the Company’s Lady Melville (1832).

The voyage includes: cook lost overboard; a suspicious felucca; a Brazilian slaver under colours; easting in 43°–45° S; fire in the galley off Sandalwood Island; Portuguese flag at Dilly; wreck-memory of Smith’s Ontario; tornado in Dampier Straits; trade with Papuan canoes near Pigeon Island and the Pelews; then Lintin after a long Pacific stretch.

First Canton; Singapore and Malacca

Landing at Jackass Point, crossing the Square into the Suy-Hong (Swedish factory), reception by Jacob Covert, Oliver H. Gordon, John H. Grosvenor, Thomas Bloodgood. Hunter is sent to study Chinese; Canton teachers proving impossible, he goes to Singapore in the Bombay ship Good Success, letters to Resident Crawford and A. L. Johnston & Co. The projected college is not ready; he is forwarded to the Anglo-Chinese College at Malacca.

Return, 1826: names on the Square

D. W. C. Olyphant has succeeded Covert; Gordon has gone to Russell & Co., No. 2 Suy-Hong. The American factory has been rebuilt. Charles N. Talbot (U.S. Consul; flag in the Square), Charles W. King, James P. Sturgis, John R. Latimer, John P. Cushing, T. T. Forbes, John Hart. Call on Rev. Dr. Morrison; examination of Malacca Chinese; tutor Le Seen-Sang. Out-of-season ships and the old Citizen still at Whampoa.

Factories, hongs, and the ground-plan

A long topographic set-piece. Factories are hired from Hong merchants. Law: no women, no guns or powder inside the gates. Lower floors: counting-rooms, godowns, compradore and coolies, a granite treasury with iron doors (no banks); payments by weight. Second floor dining and sitting; third bedrooms. Square between Old China Street and Hog Lane; Chinese forbidden to loiter; guard-house at Old China Street and the American Hong. Chop-houses of the Hoppo on the river edge: duty to stop smuggling, practice to pass silks and cloths for a fee. Factory vs Hong: foreign dwellings vs a merchant’s vast compound that can take a whole ship’s cargo.

The reference plate keys: Pwanting Qua Street, New/Old China Street, Hog Lane, the Creek, Jackass Point, Old Clothes Street, linguist Old Tom, Carpenters’ Square, custom houses, King Qua / Mou Qua / Hoa Qua hongs, Consoo House, Honam joss house, Houqua’s and Pwanting Qua’s houses, Danish/Spanish/French factories, Thirteen Factory Street, Pearl River to Macao and Whampoa. Hunter notes the later razing after Sir Michael Seymour’s bombardment.

Hong merchants, outside men, Hoppo

Hong merchants alone are officially recognised; outside merchants cannot ship except through a Hong, which takes a tax and reports to the Hoppo. Outside houses nonetheless deal on an “immense scale” in silks, matting, nankeens, crapes, grass-cloth — while edicts still list them as furnishing only “personal use” items (clothing, umbrellas, straw hats, fans, shoes). Hongs answer to the Hoppo for all export and import duties. The Hoppo is an imperial appointment, ranking with first officers of the province (Board of Revenue). Chop-boats of about 500 chests / 500 piculs; neatness of packages.

A Hong failure: a merchant sent up-country under convoy; foreign and Hong friends subscribe 10,000 dollars for his comfort — a permitted form of aid if not “excessive.”

The Honourable Company’s Factory

About twenty Company men; tea inspector Reeves; chaplain Vachell; surgeons Pierson and Colledge; interpreter Morrison. Princely hospitality: vast dining-room on the river terrace, library, billiard room; life-size George IV in robes (the picture taken by the Amherst embassy, refused at Peking, brought overland) opposite Lord Amherst. Dinner of about thirty: Bletterman of the Dutch Maatschappij; Hollingworth Magniac of Magniac & Co. (predecessors of Jardine, Matheson & Co.); Thomas Dent; Americans Wilcox and Latimer. The Company as a commercial body ends in 1833 after 250 years; Astell and Clarke remain to close accounts and leave in December 1839; political transfer of India to the Crown is dated 1858.

Opium, Lin, and the night of 24 March 1839

The memoir does not hide the traffic. Hunter says foreigners disregarded local orders and Peking, and grew confident of “perpetual impunity” in opium. Commissioner Lin (林则徐, here the Kin-Chae) demands that all opium under English merchants’ control be given up: 20,283 chests tendered; delivery at Chunpee; Alexander Johnston, Deputy Superintendent, sent 3 April. On the night of 24 March the Factories, emptied of some eight hundred Chinese servants at a moment’s notice, become “places of the dead”; foreigners cook for themselves. Hong merchants later send their own coolies to watch the gates so firewood and provisions can be brought by night. Hunter quotes a British despatch on Chinese “initiative” against life, liberty, property, and the Crown, and answers that it omits the provocation of the condemned traffic.

War years under the American flag

Active business under the American flag for English friends: teas down to Toon-Koo for England. Purchase of the English Cambridge, renamed Chesapeake, loaded with British goods valued at £150,000, last vessel into port before blockade (Volage and Hyacinth off Chunpee, 22 June 1840). Chinese raft above the second bar; later purchase of foreign ships for defence. The station ship Lintin is sent, delayed at Singapore by Capt. Townsend (lawsuit still running when Hunter leaves Macao in 1844); then sold to the authorities, towed up, eyes painted on the bows, unrigged, anchored below the Dutch Folly. Mandarins inspect; a scuttle-butt pump is taken for an “engine of war”; followers empty the cabin of gin, brandy, and biscuits. She becomes a Chinese man-of-war.

Messrs. Russell & Co., Canton, 1823–1844

Closing résumé of Kee-Chang-Hong, strictly an agency. Samuel Russell & Co. 26 Dec. 1818 – 26 Dec. 1823; Russell & Co. from 1 Jan. 1824. Partners named in terms: Russell and Philip Ammidon; William H. Low (Sumatra, 1829; dies at the Cape); Augustine Heard, Senior (bark Lintin, 1830); John C. Green, John M. Forbes, Joseph Coolidge; withdrawals 1838–39; A. A. Low; Warren Delano (from Russell, Sturgis & Co. of Canton and Manila) chief after Forbes leaves in the Niantic, 7 July 1840; Russell Sturgis 1842; King and Hunter retire 31 Dec. 1842, leave Macao Feb. 1844 in the Akbar; Sturgis retires 31 Dec. 1843. A fuller sixty-year house history by a former partner is said to exist in manuscript.

Epilogue

A “cycle” ago a gentleman boarded the Citizen at Lintin for news 125 days old. That Canton is a sealed book; the world will not see its like. Those who now seek “China Opened” are wished the same reception and protection that existed without treaties. Thanks to Robert B. Forbes. Signed W. C. H.
