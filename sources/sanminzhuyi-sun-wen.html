Three Principles of the People (Sanmin zhuyi 三民主義) — Sun Wen 孫文

What this is

Sun Wen (孫文; Sun Yat-sen / 孫中山) delivered these lectures at Guangzhou in Minguo 13 (民國十三年 / 1924) as a systematic public statement of the Three Principles of the People (Sanmin zhuyi 三民主義): nationalism (minzu zhuyi 民族主義), democracy / people’s rights (minquan zhuyi 民權主義), and people’s livelihood (minsheng zhuyi 民生主義). The present file is a collector’s edition (diancang ban 典藏版) PDF titled 三民主義(典藏版) — 孫文 — 2003 — a blue hardbound-style scan of the lecture corpus with portrait front matter, calligraphy preface page, table of contents, and the full run of lectures through the fourth minsheng lecture.

The work is not a dynastic narrative history like the companion 清史稿 / 国史大纲 / 中国通史 sources. It is a programmatic political-thought text: definitions, world comparisons, and policy claims aimed at party cadre and public audiences in the mid-1920s Guangzhou context. The 民生 series stops at four lectures in the published set — the corpus ends where the spoken series left off, not with a later PRC-era continuation.

Chapter breakdown (lecture-by-lecture)

Style: English title + (pinyin + 中文) + multi-line content gloss grounded in this edition’s openings / TOC / OCR body. Glosses track what the lecture argues, not later party doctrine.

Front matter

Portrait and seals. Guofu yixiang 國父遺像 and decorative endpapers frame the volume as a commemorative diancang object, not a bare lecture reprint.

Author’s preface (Zixu 自序). Dated 民國十三年三月三十日 at the Guangzhou Grand Headquarters (Guangzhou dabenzhen 廣州大本營). Sun presents the lectures as a corrected, extended textbook for comrades after earlier drafts and gaps; seals and calligraphy close the leaf. Positions the book as teaching material for the party’s nationalist program rather than as a finished philosophical system.

民族主義 — Nationalism

1. Nationalism, first lecture (Minzu zhuyi di yi jiang 民族主義第一講). 1924-01-27. Opens by defining ism as thought, belief, and force, and 三民主義 as 救國主義 (a doctrine to save the country). Equates Chinese minzu zhuyi with 國族主義 (nation-ism): China has family and clan loyalty but weak nation-group consciousness. Distinguishes 民族 (formed by natural “kingly way” forces) from 國家 (formed by coercive “hegemonic” force). Lists blood, livelihood, language, religion, and customs as natural binders of peoples; surveys world population pressure on China.

2. Nationalism, second lecture (di er jiang 第二講). 1924-02-03. Presses population and natural selection themes: nations rise and fall with numbers; China faces simultaneous political and economic oppression from the powers. Develops the claim that foreign banks, shipping, concessions, indemnities, and trade structure drain Chinese wealth year by year. Argues that loss of political power enables permanent economic drain — the double pressure that motivates nationalist revival.

3. Nationalism, third lecture (di san jiang 第三講). 1924-02-10. Turns to Manchu conquest methods and comparative foreign rule: how alien dynasties held China, and how that history should shape Han/Chinese national consciousness without simple revenge politics. Discusses Qing techniques of control and the need to rebuild national spirit after long subject status. Bridges historical subjugation to the present unequal-treaty order.

4. Nationalism, fourth lecture (di si jiang 第四講). 1924-02-17. Widens to world nationalism and the post–World War scene: self-determination rhetoric, the status of weak nations, and China’s place among them. Contrasts European national movements with Asian conditions. Urges Chinese nationalism as recovery of equal standing among states, not isolation.

5. Nationalism, fifth lecture (di wu jiang 第五講). 1924-02-24. Focuses on method: how to restore nationalism after it was “lost” under Qing cosmopolitan empire and treaty-port culture. Family and clan organizations as usable building blocks for larger national solidarity. Moral and organizational means — not only military force — for national revival.

6. Nationalism, sixth lecture (di liu jiang 第六講). 1924-03-02. Closes the nationalism series with cosmopolitanism vs nationalism, traditional Chinese moral claims (including datong language), and the duty of a revived China toward the world. Warns against abandoning nationalism too early under the slogan of world unity. Ends the first principle’s arc: recover the nation, then talk of broader harmony.

民權主義 — People’s rights

1. People’s rights, first lecture (Minquan zhuyi di yi jiang 民權主義第一講). 1924-03-09. Defines 政 as the affairs of the multitude and 治 as management; 民權 as the people’s power over that management. Sketches ages of struggle — man vs beast, man vs nature, man vs man — ending in the age of popular rights. Introduces democracy as the modern form of that struggle and begins comparing Chinese and Western political experience.

2. People’s rights, second lecture (di er jiang 第二講). 1924-03-16. Centers freedom: European history fought for liberty against feudal and clerical bonds; Chinese historical pain was less “unfreedom” in the European sense than loose sand disunity and later imperial/treaty oppression. Argues China should not copy Europe’s freedom slogan blindly; the need is effective national organization. Distinguishes personal license from political capacity.

3. People’s rights, third lecture (di san jiang 第三講). 1924-03-23. Centers equality: natural vs artificial equality; critiques flat equality that ignores ability. Develops the three types of people by talent (discoverers, promoters, operators) and the idea of service rather than class war as the moral of unequal gifts. Ties equality of political standing to unequal functions in administration.

4. People’s rights, fourth lecture (di si jiang 第四講). 1924-04-13. Reviews Western democracy’s progress and failures — revolutions, constitutions, party machines, and the gap between popular sovereignty in name and elite control in practice. Uses American and European examples to show where pure copied forms stall. Prepares the ground for a Chinese institutional redesign rather than simple transplant.

5. People’s rights, fifth lecture (di wu jiang 第五講). 1924-04-20. Presents the separation of power and capacity (quan neng fenshe 權能分開): the people retain four political powers (election, recall, initiative, referendum); the government exercises five governing powers (executive, legislative, judicial, examination, control) — the 五權憲法 frame. Machinery metaphors: popular control without crippling administrative competence. Core institutional lecture of the minquan series.

6. People’s rights, sixth lecture (di liu jiang 第六講). 1924-04-26. Completes the machinery discussion: how the five-power government and four popular powers mesh in practice, and how China might move from military/tutelage stages toward constitutional rule. Reinforces trained government plus sovereign people against both monarchic residue and chaotic mass politics. Closes the second principle.

民生主義 — People’s livelihood

1. People’s livelihood, first lecture (Minsheng zhuyi di yi jiang 民生主義第一講). 1924-08-03. Defines 民生 as the people’s life — food, clothing, shelter, movement — and as the center of social history. Positions minsheng against both uncritical Marxism and pure laissez-faire; discusses Marx, surplus value, and whether materialist class war fits Chinese conditions. Opens land and capital as the two great livelihood problems.

2. People’s livelihood, second lecture (di er jiang 第二講). 1924-08-10. Develops land policy: equalization of land rights (pingjun diquan 平均地權), value capture on unearned increment, and urban/rural land problems. Contrasts China’s still-limited industrial capitalism with Europe’s. Argues preventive regulation so China need not repeat Western social rupture.

3. People’s livelihood, third lecture (di san jiang 第三講). 1924-08-17. Turns to food (chifan 吃飯): agriculture, farmer livelihood, transport, and national planning for subsistence. Treats food as the first livelihood test of government. Links rural production to the wider minsheng program and to political sovereignty over economy.

4. People’s livelihood, fourth lecture (di si jiang 第四講). 1924-08-24. Centers clothing and related industrial chains (silk, cotton, wool, leather): why Chinese textile and filament industries fail under foreign political-economic pressure, tariffs, and unequal treaties. Calls for state-scale planning, customs recovery, and protection of raw and finished goods. Ends the published lecture series on industrial-political remedies for livelihood; no fifth/sixth minsheng lectures appear in this complete standard set.

Years covered

  • Spoken/written core: Minguo 13 (1924), Guangzhou — January through August lecture dates as above; preface 30 March 1924.
  • Historical references inside the text: late Qing defeats and indemnities, unequal treaties, treaty ports, comparative notes on Europe, America, Japan, Russia, and Asian peoples — used as argument, not as continuous chronicle.
  • This physical edition: 2003 collector’s reprint/scan branding in the filename; content remains the 1924 lecture canon.
  • Not covered: post-1925 institutional history of the KMT state, wartime revisions, or PRC reinterpretations — those are outside this source’s voice.

What it is useful for

  • Primary wording of Sun’s 1924 public system: definitions of the three principles, quan/neng split, five-power constitution sketch, land-value and livelihood programs.
  • Guangzhou 1924 political education context (party lectures, not a court chronicle).
  • Contrast with narrative general histories already in the wiki (清史稿, 国史大纲, 中国通史): those supply dynastic and structural story; this supplies the revolutionary-nationalist program’s self-description.
  • Tracking how unequal treaties, population, banks/shipping, and customs are framed inside nationalist economics.

What it is NOT

  • Not a complete biography of Sun Yat-sen.
  • Not a history of the 1911 Revolution, Second Revolution, or Northern Expedition (except as occasional reference).
  • Not the later full party apparatus’s commentary corpus; stick to these lectures’ claims.
  • Not a reliable character-perfect Chinese text in the OCR raw file — always check page images in _extract_sanminzhuyi/pages/ before quoting Chinese.
