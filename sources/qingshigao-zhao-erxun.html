Draft History of the Qing (Qingshi gao 清史稿)

Zhao Erxun (Zhao Erxun 趙爾巽 / 赵尔巽) and the Qing History Bureau (Qingshi guan 清史馆)
Republican draft dynastic history of the Qing house · standard 529 juan (volumes) · Chinese (classical historiographic prose)
EPUB digital object (2011 package; Anna’s Archive id in filename) · immutable extract wiki/raw/qingshigao-zhao-erxun.txt

Chinese names below are given in English or standard romanization first, with characters (and pinyin when helpful) in parentheses on first use in a section. Reign temple names keep the usual English forms (Kangxi, Qianlong, etc.) with Chinese once.

What this book is

This is the Qingshi gao (清史稿, Draft History of the Qing) — compiled under the Republican Qingshi guan (清史馆, Qing History Bureau), with Zhao Erxun (趙爾巽) as the name tradition attaches to the bureau’s work. It is not a private essay and not a finished zhengshi (正史, orthodox/standard history) certified by a later empire; the title itself marks a gao (稿, draft) dynastic history written in the received fourfold shape of the standard histories:

  1. Benji (本纪, imperial annals)
  2. Zhi (志, treatises on institutions and knowledge)
  3. Biao (表, chronological and genealogical tables)
  4. Liezhuan (列传, biographies and collective lives)

ending in long monographs on tusi (土司, native/aboriginal chieftaincies), fanbu (籓部 / 藩部, inner feudatories), and shuguo (属国, dependent/attached states).

A reader who has never opened the book will find, in Chinese of the official-historiographic register, a continuous account of the Qing as the bureau reconstructed it: how the Aisin Gioro (爱新觉罗) rose in the Jianzhou (建州) marches; how the Later Jin and then the Qing took the north and the Ming capital; how successive reigns ordered ritual, revenue, arms, law, and letters; how governors, banners, and feudatories staffed the realm; and how the house’s last generations met foreign residence, new offices, traffic by steam and wire, and the close of the imperial line under the Xuantong (宣统) emperor. The digital filing used here is a complete standard object of that draft — benji 25 + zhi 135 + biao 53 + liezhuan 316 = 529 juan — with continuous prose and tables through the spine, not a table-of-contents shell.

The present source page does not judge the bureau’s politics or later scholarly disputes over the draft. It narrates what the text contains, so that a reader without Chinese, and without the volumes open, can see the history the book itself lays out.

Scope and horizon

Beginning. The first annals open on the house before the dynastic name is settled: the Jianzhou (建州) setting, the Aisin Gioro line, and Taizu (太祖) Nurhaci (Nuerhachi 努尔哈齐). The body of the Taizu benji (太祖本纪) moves from the sexagenary year guiwei (癸未) — equated in the text with Ming Wanli 11 (明万历十一年, 1583) — when Nurhaci raises forces against Nikan Wailan (尼堪外兰) after the deaths associated with Tulun city (图伦城), through conquest and alliance among Jurchen groupings, the building of a capital and a banner order, and the proclamation of the Tianming (天命, “Heavenly Mandate”) era and a state that contemporaries and the draft treat as the foundation of the later Qing. Early pages are thick with place-names of the northeast, fortress takes, marriages, and the naming of the regime.

Middle. Through Taizong (太宗), Shizu (世祖), and the long High- and mid-Qing reigns the annals run year by year (or campaign by campaign) over accession, regency, war, disaster, sacrifice, personnel, and foreign or tributary contact. The treatises freeze institutions into topical monographs; the tables pin ranks, fiefs, and postings to time; the biographies multiply individual and grouped lives.

End. The last annals are the Xuantong huangdi benji (宣统皇帝本纪, Annals of the Xuantong Emperor). Late treatises include matter the older Twenty-Four Histories did not carry in the same form — jiaotong (交通, traffic and communications: posts, roads, steam, wire) and bangjiao (邦交, foreign intercourse with named states) — and the tables of jiaopin (交聘, diplomatic exchange / residencies) list resident envoys sent and received. The biographical division closes on model officials, scholars, writers, the loyal and the filial, recluses, artists, mathematical astronomers, women of note, then six juan of tusi, eight of fanbu, and four of shuguo.

A sample of Shuguo IV (属国四, Dependent States, juan 4) opens on the Gurkhas (Kuoerka 廓尔喀 — Nepal) relative to Ü-Tsang / Wei-Zang (卫藏, central Tibet as the draft names it), the absorption of Balbu (巴勒布) polities, and bounds toward Sikkim (Zhemengxiong 哲孟雄) and Bhutan (Bulukeba 布鲁克巴), then carries a heading-cluster of Central and Inner Asian names — among them Kokand (Haohan 浩罕), Burut / Kyrgyz (Bulute 布鲁特), Kazakh (Hasake 哈萨克), Andijan (Anjiyan 安集延), and others listed below under Dependent States — with narrative that includes late Qianlong-era conflict on the Tibetan frontier. The book’s object is the Qing dynasty in the bureau’s frame — not pre-Qin antiquity, and not Republican party politics after 1912, except where late annals, bangjiao, jiaopin, and shuguo touch the dynasty’s last external relations.

How the work is ordered

Like other standard histories, the draft does not tell one novel from page one to the end. It offers four concurrent instruments:

  1. Benji (本纪) — the emperor’s reign as the spine of public time: accessions, edicts, campaigns, rites, portents, and the deaths and appointments the bureau thought belonged in the sovereign’s record.
  2. Zhi (志) — systematic monographs on sky and calendar, geography, ritual and music, dress, examinations, offices, food and goods, rivers, war, law, literature, traffic, and foreign intercourse.
  3. Biao (表) — compressed time-grids of empresses and consorts, imperial princes, banner and civil noble houses, Grand Council ministers, board presidents, governors-general and governors, frontier generals, feudatory genealogies, and diplomatic residencies.
  4. Liezhuan (列传) — lives: first consorts and princes, then the long central run of officials and commanders, then classified terminal series and the three frontier/feudatory monographic blocks.

A single event (a war, a river breach, a treaty) may appear in the annals under its year, again in a treatise under its institution, again in a table as a posting, and again in the biography of a commander. The source page below follows the book’s own order so a reader can see what each large block is for.

Part I — Imperial annals (benji 本纪), 25 juan

The annals are twenty-five juan. They are not evenly thick: long reigns occupy several juan; short or late reigns may share or compress. What the reader meets is reign-ordered political narrative in classical annalistic style — not modern chapter essays.

Taizu (太祖) — Nurhaci (努尔哈齐)

The opening annals recount the pre-dynastic and dynastic founding of the house. After a genealogical and geographic preface (Jianzhou, the Aisin Gioro line, relations with Ming frontier structures), the narrative turns on guiwei (癸未) / Wanli 11 (万历十一年, 1583): Nurhaci’s rising against Nikan Wailan (尼堪外兰), the struggle over fortresses such as Tulun city (图伦城), the gathering of allies and the destruction of rivals among Jurchen groups, the organization of military following that becomes the Eight Banners (baqi 八旗) system, the choice of capitals, and the declaration of Tianming (天命) as era-name and of a state that the draft treats as the root of the Qing. The texture is names, places, marriages, submissions, and battles — the bureau’s origin story of the dynasty in the northeast before entry into China proper.

Taizong (太宗)

The Taizong annals continue state-building and external war after Nurhaci: further ordering of banners and offices, campaigns and negotiations toward Ming and toward Korean (Chaoxian 朝鲜) and Mongol theatres, and the transition of titles and capital practice that prepare the later entry into the Ming domains. The reader follows a conquest polity consolidating its claim to rule, still centered on the northeast and the steppe–Ming frontier, not yet the Beijing court of the later annals.

Shizu (世祖) — entry into the pass; Dorgon; early Qing at Beijing

The Shizu annals cover the reign of the emperor known in temple style as Shizu (the Shunzhi emperor in era-name usage): the moment the Qing take the Ming capital and begin to govern China proper. The prose carries the passage through the passes, the role of Dorgon (Duo’ergun 多尔衮) and other princes in regency and conquest, the installation of Qing rule at Beijing, the pursuit of Ming loyalist resistance in the south and along coasts and rivers, and the early settlement of banner garrisons, examinations, and metropolitan offices. For a reader, this is the hinge from a regional khanal-dynastic state to an empire seated on the Ming institutional map.

Shengzu (圣祖) — the Kangxi reign (Kangxi 康熙)

Several juan belong to the long Kangxi reign. The annals move year by year through minority and personal rule, the suppression of the Three Feudatories (sanfan 三藩) and related coastal and southern wars, campaigns toward Taiwan (Taiwan 台湾) and the maritime fringe, northern and northwestern war and treaty horizons (including the Russian frontier as the draft registers it), the ordering of the Yellow River (Huanghe 黄河) and Grand Canal (Dayunhe 大运河 / caoyun 漕运 system) as recurrent imperial concerns, literary and encyclopedic projects as they enter the court record, and the long management of princes, favorites, and succession. Disaster, eclipse, sacrifice, and personnel run in the same annalistic breath as war: the bureau’s Kangxi is a reign of consolidation after conquest, written as continuous sovereign time rather than as a modern thematic biography.

Shizong (世宗) — the Yongzheng reign (Yongzheng 雍正)

The Yongzheng annals are shorter in juan count than Kangxi or Qianlong but dense in administrative texture. They carry the succession after Kangxi, the emperor’s tightening of central document flow and secret memorial culture as the draft records it, fiscal and cadastral pressure, the handling of princes and cliques, northwestern and southwestern military and administrative pushes, and the moral-legal tone of edicts that later reigns inherit. A reader meets a reign framed as disciplinary reform of the conquest elite and the Han bureaucracy alike.

Gaozong (高宗) — the Qianlong reign (Qianlong 乾隆)

The Qianlong annals are among the thickest blocks in the benji. They run a very long reign: decades of campaigns the court counted among its major wars (western and southern fronts, Jinchuan (金川), Myanmar border affairs, Taiwan, Gurkha/Tibet, and others as the annals assign them), the incorporation and reordering of Huibu (回部, the Muslim/Tarim oasis regions in Qing administrative language) and other western spaces, enormous ritual and tour activity, literary inquisition and huge editorial projects as they appear in the sovereign record, favoritism and late-reign corruption crises, and the emperor’s abdication calendar in favor of the next reign while power still hangs on the retired emperor. Samples of mid-Qianlong years in the extract show the texture clearly: banner and Green Standard (lüying 绿营) transfers, river labor at Qingkou (清口) and related nodes, Korean and Ryukyu (Liuqiu 琉球) tributary missions, appointments across boards and provinces, and coded moral praise and blame of officials — the everyday machine of High Qing court time.

Renzong (仁宗) — the Jiaqing reign (Jiaqing 嘉庆)

The Jiaqing annals open a reign already under strain. The reader finds the death of Qianlong, the removal of late favorites, White Lotus (Bailian 白莲) and related uprisings and their suppression costs, piracy and coastal trouble, continued river and famine administration, and a court trying to restore order after the High Qing peak. The tone of the annals remains imperial and year-ordered; the content shifts toward internal unrest and fiscal fatigue.

Xuanzong (宣宗) — the Daoguang reign (Daoguang 道光)

Daoguang annals carry the dynasty into sharper foreign and fiscal pressure. Opium, maritime trade conflict, and the wars and settlements that follow appear in the sovereign record alongside ordinary personnel, rites, and disasters. Northwestern Muslim rebellions and other interior crises interleave with coastal diplomacy. For the reader of the draft, this reign is where “overseas” powers become a standing problem inside the annals, not only an item for later bangjiao treatises.

Wenzong (文宗) — the Xianfeng reign (Xianfeng 咸丰)

Xianfeng annals are dominated by the Taiping (太平) rebellion and concurrent northern and southern wars, the flight of the court when allied forces approach the capital, the new treaty regime, and the death of the emperor away from Beijing. Banner and provincial armies, irregulars, and the rise of Han provincial commanders enter the annals as the military ground shifts under the throne.

Muzong (穆宗) — the Tongzhi reign (Tongzhi 同治)

Tongzhi annals cover a child emperorship and regency under empresses dowager, the gradual suppression of Taiping, Nian (捻), and major Muslim rebellions, the early Self-Strengthening arsenals and offices as they appear in court time, and residual foreign residence and indemnity structures. The sovereign is often ceremonial in the prose; the political content sits in edicts, appointments, and campaign reports.

Dezong (德宗) — the Guangxu reign (Guangxu 光绪)

Guangxu annals run from another child accession through the empress dowager’s dominance, the Sino-Japanese war and its settlements, reform edicts and their reversal, the Boxer crisis and allied expedition, the late xinzheng (新政, “new policies”) program, and the emperor’s death. Institutional novelty — ministries on foreign models, schools, police, provincial consultative bodies — appears in the annals as appointments and edicts, and is expanded in the late zhiguan (职官, offices), xuanju (选举, selection/exams), jiaotong, and bangjiao treatises.

Xuantong emperor (Xuantong huangdi benji 宣统皇帝本纪)

The final annals are the Xuantong (宣统) reign: a child emperor, regency, continued new-policy restructuring, railway and constitutional crises as the draft records them, provincial separation, the 1911 uprisings, abdication edicts, and the close of Qing sovereign time. The reader finishes the benji not with Republican party history but with the bureau’s record of how the imperial line ends.

Across all twenty-five juan the method is the same: reign title + year, then compact entries. Modern chapter titles are rare; meaning is in the accumulation of campaigns, rites, floods, stars, and names.

Part II — Treatises (zhi 志), 135 juan

The treatises are the draft’s institutional memory. They do not replace the annals; they lift recurrent machinery out of year-time into topical monographs. Category counts in this filing follow the received Qingshi gao set.

Astronomy (tianwen 天文), 14 juan

These juan treat the sky as the bureau’s astronomical office recorded it: lunar and planetary mansions, polar and stellar divisions in Chinese uranography, anomalous appearances, and the link between heaven’s signs and the court’s obligation to observe and fix the calendar world. A reader finds technical star-and-sign prose, not a popular star myth book — continuous with earlier dynastic tianwen zhi (天文志).

Disasters and anomalies (zaiyi 灾异), 5 juan

A catalogue-treatise of earthquakes, floods, droughts, locusts, prodigies, and other disasters and omens, arranged so that the realm’s suffering and portents can be read apart from the annals’ year-entries. Useful to the bureau as moral-political weather; useful to a modern reader as a compressed disaster register across the dynasty.

Time and calendar systems (shixian 时宪), 9 juan

Shixian carries the calendar and mathematical-astronomical system of the Qing — the Shixian li (时宪历, “Timely Models” calendar) tradition and its underlying computational frames, including the engagement with Jesuit and later European methods as absorbed into official calendar work. The reader meets epoch constants, month-intercalation logic, and the institutional claim that correct time is a sovereign duty.

Geography (dili 地理), 28 juan

One of the largest treatise blocks. It lays out the Qing map in administrative language: metropolitan and provincial circuits, prefectures and counties, frontier generalships and dependency spaces, changes of name and seat across reigns, distances and bounds as the bureau fixed them. Reading it is like reading a dynastic gazetteer fused to a history of jurisdictional reform — from the early Qing inheritance of Ming units through High Qing expansion and late Qing new provinces and treaty-port scars as geography records them.

Rites (li 礼), 12 juan

Sacrifices at suburban altars and temples, ancestral rites, court ceremony, investiture, mourning, guest ritual, and the classification of ritual cases. The Qing claim to have ordered All-under-Heaven is written here as a code of performances the emperor and bureaucracy owe heaven, ancestors, and envoys.

Music (yue 乐), 8 juan

Court music, pitch standards, hymn texts, and the musical establishment — the aural half of ritual sovereignty. Instruments, modes, and institutional offices for music appear as historical monograph, including reform and antiquarian recovery across reigns.

Carriages and dress (yufu 舆服), 4 juan

Sumptuary and ceremonial regulation of vehicles, banners, crowns, robes, and rank marks — how status was made visible in motion and clothing for emperor, princes, officials, and others the code names.

Examinations and selection (xuanju 选举), 8 juan

The ladder of schools, exams, and alternative routes into office: keju (科举, civil service examination) sessions and tiers, special examinations, recommendation, purchase and contribution routes as the draft admits them, and late-Qing school and abroad-study channels that begin to displace the classical exam world. A reader sees both the High Qing exam monarchy and its late unraveling into “new education.”

Offices (zhiguan 职官), 6 juan

The tree of government: Grand Secretariat (neige 内阁) and Grand Council (junjichu 军机处), the Six Boards (liubu 六部) and their late successors, Censorate, banners and the Eight Banner offices, provincial governors-general (zongdu 总督) and governors (xunfu 巡抚), judicial and salt and grain posts, and the xin guanzhi (新官制, new official system) band that records late-Qing ministry reform. This is the prose map of who was supposed to do what, complementary to the personnel biao.

Food and goods (shihuo 食货), 6 juan

Land and population registration, taxes in kind and silver, tribute grain (caoliang 漕粮), salt, coinage and monetary troubles, warehouses, and related economic administration. For economic structure as the bureau understood it, these juan are the core monograph — always in the language of official supply and control, not of later economic theory.

Rivers and canals (hequ 河渠), 4 juan

The Yellow River, the Grand Canal, Huai (淮) and related systems, dikes, breaches, transport of tribute grain, and the offices of river administration. Recurrent catastrophe and repair from the annals are here systematized as hydraulic history.

Military (bing 兵), 12 juan

Eight Banners and Green Standard establishment, provincial armies and braves as they enter the official story, training, weapons, garrisons, military finance, and late new armies and navy fragments. The treatise form lets the reader see force structure apart from individual campaigns in the annals and lives in the biographies.

Law (xingfa 刑法), 3 juan

Codes, substatutes, punishments, revision across reigns, and judicial offices — the Qing legal order as a historical monograph, including the pressure of new crimes and foreign consular regimes at the end.

Bibliography (yiwen 艺文), 4 juan

A classified record of books and compilations the bureau attributed to the dynasty’s literary corpus — classics, history, masters, collections — in the tradition of dynastic bibliographical treatises. It is a map of titles and categories, not plot summaries of each work.

Traffic and communications (jiaotong 交通), 4 juan

A late-style treatise: routes, posts, shipping, railroads, telegraph and related infrastructure as the Qing state built, conceded, or regulated them. Together with bangjiao and late xuanju / zhiguan, this block marks the draft as a history that must absorb nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century material older standard histories never framed this way.

Foreign intercourse (bangjiao 邦交), 8 juan

Monographs of relations with named foreign states and groups as the bureau ordered them — not only the classical gong (贡, tribute) framework but treaty powers and neighbor states of the late empire. Read beside the jiaopin nianbiao (交聘年表, diplomatic residency year-tables) and the shuguo biographies, this is the draft’s external-relations encyclopedia.

Part III — Tables (biao 表), 53 juan

The tables are not narrative chapters. They are grids and genealogical streams that pin people and posts to time. A reader uses them to answer “who held what, when,” and “how a house or fief descended.”

  • Empresses and consorts (houfei biao 后妃表) — marital and rank chronology of the inner court.
  • Imperial princes (huangzi / zhuwang 皇子 / 诸王 related tables) — sons of emperors and princely lines as the bureau tabulated them.
  • Banner and noble houses (zhuchen fengjue shibiao 诸臣封爵世表) — hereditary ranks among meritorious and banner lineages across generations.
  • Grand Council ministers (junji dachen nianbiao 军机大臣年表) — year-ordered occupancy of the dynasty’s core executive council.
  • Board and metropolitan ministers (buyuan dachen nianbiao 部院大臣年表) — presidents and related high posts of the boards and courts, often in upper/lower (上/下) paired juan.
  • Provincial and frontier posts (jiangchen nianbiao 疆臣年表) — governors-general (including river and grain special posts), governors, and frontier generals, lieutenants, and high ministers of the marches.
  • Feudatory genealogies (fanbu shibiao 籓部世表) — ruling houses of inner feudatories in tabular descent.
  • Diplomatic residencies (jiaopin nianbiao 交聘年表) — two juan of the late pattern: Chinese envoys abroad and foreign envoys in China.

Grand secretaries (daxueshi 大学士) and coadjutors appear densely in annals and biographies even where a standalone daxueshi nianbiao (大学士年表) header is thin in the ebook navigation; entity work should read the junji and buyuan tables together with benji appointments rather than trusting the sidebar alone.

Part IV — Biographies (liezhuan 列传), 316 juan

The biographical division is more than half the book. It opens with the inner court and the blood elite, runs a vast central sequence of officials and commanders, then closes with moral and occupational categories and three monographic blocks on the non-prefectural periphery.

Consorts (houfei 后妃) — Liezhuan I (列传一)

Lives of empresses and consorts: family origin, rank rises and falls, motherhood of heirs, political intervention where the bureau records it, and death rites. This is the inner-court companion to the consort tables.

Princes (zhuwang 诸王) — Liezhuan II–VIII (列传二–八)

Seven juan of imperial princes and princely houses — merits in conquest, fiefs, scandals, degradations, and branches of the Aisin Gioro and associated lines. Early juan lean on founding brothers and sons; later material tracks enfeoffed lines through the dynasty.

Central run of officials and commanders — roughly Liezhuan IX through the low 260s

Here the draft stacks individual and grouped biographies in the bureau’s sequence: early conquerors and collaborators, High Qing grand secretaries and generals, mid-Qing administrators, suppression commanders of the nineteenth century, statesmen of Self-Strengthening and reform, and figures of the close. Many mid-juan have only bare Liezhuan N labels in the ebook nav; inside the prose, lives are person-titled. The language of the corpus also knows condemnatory or liminal classes — erchen (贰臣, officials who served two dynasties), nichen (逆臣, rebellious ministers), jianchen (奸臣, treacherous ministers), panchen (叛臣, turncoat ministers) and related labels — used when the bureau sorts moral types; exact juan cuts for those labels should be fixed when those fascicles are opened for entity pages. For a reader, this long middle is the human grain of Qing government and war: not one plot, but hundreds of careers that together enact the annals.

Model officials (xunli 循吏)

Several juan of officials praised for upright local administration — the draft’s gallery of “followable officials,” useful as the bureau’s ideal of governance rather than as a statistical sample of all magistrates.

Confucian scholars (rulin 儒林)

Lives of classical scholars and intellectual lineages as the bureau recognized them — evidential research, school affiliation, works, and official or teaching careers.

Literary figures (wenyuan 文苑)

Writers and literary reputations: poets, prose masters, and men of letters whose fame the draft places under letters rather than under pure official merit.

Loyalty and righteousness (zhongyi 忠义)

A long block (on the order of ten juan) of those who died or suffered for loyalty — especially thick for conquest, Ming–Qing transition, and nineteenth-century wars and rebellions. Collective entries and local mass deaths appear beside famous names.

Filiality and duty (xiaoyi 孝义)

Exemplars of filial piety and kindred virtues — moral biography continuous with earlier dynastic histories’ didactic collections.

Remnant recluses (yiyi 遗逸)

Men who withdrew or refused service — including tones of Ming remnant loyalty and later eremitic choice — as the bureau chose to remember them.

Arts (yishu 艺术)

Painters, calligraphers, physicians, and other technical-artistic lives that sit outside the pure scholar-official ladder.

Mathematical astronomers (chouren 畴人)

Specialists in calendar, calculation, and related exact knowledge — a thin but pointed continuation of the chouren tradition, to be read beside shixian and tianwen.

Women (lienü 列女)

Four juan of women exemplary for chastity, martyrdom, talent, or other virtues the bureau’s category admits — a didactic female companion to zhongyi and xiaoyi.

Aboriginal offices (tusi 土司), six juan

Not brief epitaphs but a regional-administrative monograph series: native chieftaincies of the southwest and related zones, their genealogies, submissions, rebellions, and conversion toward direct rule (gaitu guiliu 改土归流, replacing native chieftains with circulating appointed officials) as the draft narrates it. Geography and surname-polities dominate.

Feudatories (fanbu 籓部), eight juan

Inner Asian and related feudatory polities under Qing suzerainty — Mongol leagues and khans, Tibetan and other highland structures as classified here, and the institutional story of titles, tribute, and intervention. Read with fanbu shibiao and dili.

Dependent states (shuguo 属国), four juan

The outer ring of “attached states” in the bureau’s language. Earlier shuguo juan treat long-standing partners as the bureau ordered them — notably Korea (Chaoxian 朝鲜), Ryukyu (Liuqiu 琉球), Vietnam (Annan / Yuenan 安南 / 越南), and related Southeast and East Asian partners where the draft places them.

What a continuous reader learns (without reading every juan)

Taken as one bureau project, the Qingshi gao offers:

  • A sovereign-time history of the Qing from Nurhaci’s rise and the Tianming (天命) state to the Xuantong (宣统) abdication, in annalistic grit rather than modern chapter synthesis.
  • An institutional encyclopedia of how the dynasty claimed to know heaven and time, map territory, stage ritual, recruit officials, tax and move grain, fight, punish, print and classify books, lay rails and wires, and deal with foreign states.
  • A personnel machine in tables: who sat on the Grand Council, who governed provinces, how noble and feudatory houses descended.
  • A biographical nation: consorts, princes, the working elite, moral exemplars, specialists, women of the didactic categories, and the native (tusi), feudatory (fanbu), and dependent (shuguo) peripheries.

It does not replace local gazetteers, private memoirs, archival streams, or foreign-language sources; it is the late, official-style summa of the dynasty written after the dynasty fell, in the shape of the standard histories.

Completeness of this digital object

Complete as a standard Qingshi gao filing: all four divisions are present at the received counts (25 + 135 + 53 + 316 = 529). The extract is on the order of 5.3 million characters of continuous classical prose and tables; spine documents are non-empty. Narrative is verified at the founding end (Taizu / Tianming arc) and in late matter (Xuantong annals present; shuguo with real monograph texture). Late treatises (jiaotong, bangjiao, xin guanzhi inside offices, jiaopin tables) confirm the object runs through the dynasty’s final institutional layer.

Caveats (properties of the work or the package, not silent truncation):

  1. Gao, not zhengshi (稿, not 正史) — a Republican bureau draft; unfinished certification is in the title.
  2. Ebook navigation is thinner than the body — many zhi juan and most mid-liezhuan lack human titles in the NCX; content remains in the raw extract. Do not treat missing sidebar labels as missing juan.
  3. Orthography — mixed modern simplified/traditional typesetting typical of digital Qingshi gao packages; not a diplomatic transcript of one 1928 print copy.
  4. Secondary directory residue near the file end is navigation debris, not a second incomplete book.

Bottom line: fit foundation source for Qing-wide work in this wiki. It does not by itself cover pre-Qing antiquity or post-imperial politics except where late external-relations matter touches them.

Note on this page’s method

Earlier draft of this source page leaned on division counts and category tables (imperial annals / treatises / tables / biography labels). That scaffolding is true but not what a reader needs first. Following the Rest of Europe 1800–65 pattern (e.g. German and Russian multi-volume sources), this page narrates the contents in English: what each large block of the book actually walks through, with concrete anchors from the extract (guiwei / Wanli 11 and Nikan Wailan; Dorgon and entry into the pass; mid-Qianlong year-texture; shixian and bangjiao/jiaotong as institutional horizons; Shuguo IV on Gurkha/Nepal and Central Asian names). Chinese characters remain where they identify the source’s own labels; they are paired with romanization and, where stable, common English names so a non-reader of the book is not left with untranslated strings. Juan counts remain as orientation, not as a substitute for summary.
